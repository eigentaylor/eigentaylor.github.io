A Quick Look at Sen's Theorem

A brief alternate proof of the theorem that individual sovereignty and efficiency are incompatible.

Introduction

I may do a much more in-depth post on Sen’s theorem in the future, but for now I just want to give a quick overview of the theorem with a different example than the one Sen uses in his original paper.

Among Arrow’s theorem and the theorems of Gibbard, Sen’s theorem is often overlooked. It is a bit of the “red-headed stepchild” of social choice theory and is often overshadowed by the more famous theorems of Arrow and Gibbard. As a voting theorist, Sen’s theorem is… a bit less consequential than Arrow’s or Gibbard’s theorems.

Arrow and Gibbard deal with IIA and strategyproofness, respectively, which are themselves very desirable properties. Their impossibility theorems essentially say that these desirable properties are too restrictive to be satisfied by any reasonable voting system. Sen’s theorem, which deals with “minimal liberalism,” is… less obviously desirable.

Minimal Liberalism

Definition: (Minimal Liberalism) A social welfare function (a function which takes in individual preferences and outputs a social preference order) satisfies minimal liberalism if there exist at least two individuals who are “decisive” over some pair of alternatives.

Intuitively, this captures a kind of “individual rights.” If I am one of these “decisive” individuals, we might interpret this as follows:

If I have the freedom of speech, then I am decisive over the pair of alternatives “speak” (S) and “be quiet” (Q). Therefore, if I prefer to speak (prefer $S>Q$), then the social preference order will reflect my preference. That is, if I rank $S>Q$, then society must rank $S>Q$ as well.

Another way to interpret this is that I have a kind of “veto power” over the pair of alternatives $S$ and $Q$. If I prefer $S>Q$, then I absolutely prevent $Q$ from being the top alternative in the social preference order.

Similarly, if I choose to prefer $Q>S$, then I force the social preference order to reflect that as well (i.e. $Q>S$ in the social preference order). In this way, I can also “veto” $S$ from being the top alternative in the social preference order. What I say goes! At least for the pair of alternatives I am decisive over.

We can associate a social welfare function with a social choice function (a function which takes in individual preferences and outputs a single winning alternative) by defining the social choice function to select the top-ranked alternative in the social preference order. Therefore, if I am decisive over $S$ and $Q$, and I prefer $S>Q$, then the social choice function can never output $Q$.

By saying I prefer $S>Q$, I may not guarantee that $S$ is the top-ranked alternative in the social preference order, but I can guarantee that $Q$ is certainly not. Hence, the idea of “veto power”.

Pareto Efficiency

The other important concept in Sen’s theorem is “Pareto efficiency”.

Definition: (Pareto Efficiency) A social welfare function satisfies Pareto efficiency if, whenever every individual prefers alternative $A$ to alternative $B$, the social preference order also ranks $A$ above $B$.

Another way to think of efficiency is that if there is a “Pareto improvement” (a strictly better alternative that everyone prefers), then we should never choose the worse alternative. If we have a case where everyone prefers $A$ to $B$, and we somehow end up with $B$ as the top-ranked alternative in the social preference order, then that’s sufficient to say that our social welfare function is not Pareto efficient.

The final two concepts we need to understand Sen’s theorem are “unrestricted domain” and “transitivity.” Unrestricted domain just means that we allow individuals to have any preference order they want (no ballots or preferences are illegal). Transitivity means that if $A>B$ and $B>C$, then we must have $A>C$. Simple, right?

Sen’s theorem can be interpreted as saying that if we have a social welfare function that satisfies minimal liberalism, and we have “unrestricted domain” and “transitivity”, then we cannot have Pareto efficiency. This is not the typical statement of Sen’s theorem, but it is sufficient to prove the theorem. The typical statement is

Theorem: (Sen’s Theorem) There is no social welfare function that satisfies minimal liberalism, Pareto efficiency, unrestricted domain, and transitivity.

Like Arrow’s theorem, it’s of the form “you can’t have all of these things at once”. But also like Arrow’s theorem, the proof is generally structured as showing one of the properties is violated if we have all the others. In this case, we will show that if we have minimal liberalism, unrestricted domain, and transitivity, then we cannot have Pareto efficiency.

Intuitively, this is a relatively intuitive idea. If we have to respect individual rights (minimal liberalism), then we might be left with an outcome which is clearly not optimal.

Proof by Example

We are going to prove Sen’s theorem through an example. I personally found Sen’s original example to… not really make a lot of sense. So we are going to go through a different example that I think is a bit more intuitive, and more closely relates to the idea of “individual rights” that Sen is trying to capture with minimal liberalism.

We have two individuals, Clark the Conspiracy Theorist ($C$) and Seth the Skeptic ($S$), who work together in the same office. Clark is a strong believer that the Earth is flat, and he wants to tell Seth all about it. Clark has the right to free speech, so he is decisive over the pair of alternatives

  • $T$: “Clark Tells Seth about the flat Earth.” Clark loves to debate people about the flat Earth, and he wants to convince Seth of the “truth”.
  • $Q$: “Clark, specifically, must keep Quiet and be silenced about the flat Earth.” He is not allowed to talk about the flat Earth to anyone in the office, though other people in the office still might tell Seth about it.

Seth, on the other hand, has little patience for conspiracy theories, and he just wants to be left alone. He believes he has the right not to be offended by nonsense, so he is decisive over the pair of alternatives

  • $N$: “Never hear about the flat Earth from anyone in the office.” Clark would be allowed to talk about the flat Earth to other people in the workplace, just not to Seth.
  • $T$: “Clark Tells Seth about the flat Earth.” Seth can choose to allow Clark to debate him about the flat Earth.

We assume that Seth’s right not to be offended by nonsense means that he is also decisive over the pair $N$ and $T$. But he can’t force Clark to be quiet ($Q$) and not tell anyone else in the office about the flat Earth, so he is not decisive over $Q$.

Clark, however, is not decisive over $N$ because he doesn’t have the right to prevent or force other people to talk about the flat Earth to Seth. He only has the right to decide whether he, himself, talks about it.

This is where Minimal Liberalism starts to run into problems: when people’s individual rights conflict with each other. Clark has the right to free speech, which means he can choose (be decisive) either to be silenced about the flat Earth ($Q$) or to speak about it to Seth ($T$). Seth has the right not to be offended by nonsense, which means he should be able to choose (be decisive) either never to hear about the flat Earth from anyone in the office ($N$) or to hear about it from Clark ($T$). But these rights are in direct conflict with each other.

Intuitively, we have an innate sense that the freedom of speech and the right to not be offended by nonsense are contradictory. We are going to mathematically prove that if we must respect both of these rights, then we will end up with a non-optimal outcome.

Let’s suppose Seth comes to Human Resources and complains about Clark. HR brings Seth and Clark into the room and tries to figure out what to do. They have to choose between the three alternatives $T$, $Q$, and $N$. HR wants to protect Clark’s freedom of speech, but they also want to protect Seth’s right not to be offended by nonsense.

Clark has the preference that, most of all, he wants to tell Seth about the flat Earth ($T$). And he also absolutely hates the idea that Seth might never hear about the flat Earth from someone else ($N$). He is not a fan of being quiet ($Q$), but it’s not as bad as Seth never hearing about the flat Earth. Clark believes the truth must come out! So his preference order is $T > Q > N$.

Seth, on the other hand, would like never to have to hear about the flat Earth from anyone at work ($N$). However, he is also very principled. He hates misinformation, so he would actually prefer that Clark never spread the flat Earth conspiracy theory at all in the workplace ($Q$). It’s just not appropriate! Thus, he actually prefers $Q$ to $N$. But, most of all, he hates the idea of Clark telling him specifically about the flat Earth ($T$). So his preference order is $Q > N > T$.

We are thus left with

Person Preference Order
Clark $T > Q > N$
Seth $Q > N > T$

We now look at the alternatives that each person is decisive over. Clark is decisive over $T$ and $Q$, so if he prefers $T>Q$, then we must have $T>Q$ in the social preference order. Seth is decisive over $T$ and $N$, so if he prefers $N>T$, then we must have $N>T$ in the social preference order. Therefore, we have $N>T$ and $T>Q$ in the social preference order.

By transitivity, if we have $N>T$ and $T>Q$, then we must have $N>Q$ in the social preference order. That is, society must rank $N>T>Q$. The social choice must, then, be $N$.

From the veto perspective, we have the following:

Person Decisive Pair Vetoed Alternative
Clark $T>Q$ $Q$
Seth $N>T$ $T$

We can’t ever choose $Q$, because Clark’s freedom of speech allows him to veto being silenced ($Q$). We also can’t ever choose $T$, because Seth’s right to not be offended by nonsense allows him to veto being told about the flat Earth from Clark specifically ($T$). Therefore, we are forced to choose $N$: Seth won’t have to hear about the flat Earth from anyone in the office.

Therefore, to respect both of their rights, the HR department decides to impose $N$: a rule that Seth is not to hear about the flat Earth from anyone in the office, while Clark is still allowed to talk about it to other people in the office (but only if Seth doesn’t hear about it).

Here’s where we run into the problem: Both Clark and Seth prefer $Q$ to $N$. That is, if forced to choose between $Q$ and $N$, both Clark and Seth would prefer $Q$. Thus, there’s actually a better alternative that both Clark and Seth would be happier with.

Clark got his worst choice: Seth won’t hear about the flat Earth from anyone in the office ($N$). He would be happier if he, specifically, had to keep quiet about the flat Earth ($Q$), but others in the office were still allowed to talk about it.

Seth is moderately happy: he won’t have to hear about the flat Earth from anyone while at work ($N$), but he would prefer it if Clark were banned from talking about the flat Earth to anyone in the office ($Q$).

Therefore, we have an outcome ($N$) that is Pareto dominated by another outcome ($Q$). By forcing ourselves to respect both of their rights, and satisfy minimal liberalism, we end up with a non-Pareto efficient outcome. This is the essence of Sen’s theorem.

The Cycle Perspective

Another way to conclude the proof is to necessarily impose Pareto efficiency, and then show that we end up with a cycle in the social preference order (contradicting transitivity). If we force Pareto efficiency, then we must absolutely have $Q>N$ in the social preference order. We thus have that

  • $Q>N$ (by Pareto efficiency)
  • $T>Q$ (by Clark’s liberal decisiveness)
  • $N>T$ (by Seth’s liberal decisiveness)

which creates a cycle.

Alternatively, by the veto perspective, we can say that Pareto efficiency vetoes $N$ (by forcing $Q$ over $N$). Thus,

Vetoed Alternative Reason
$T$ Seth’s liberalism on $T>N$
$Q$ Clark’s liberalism on $T>Q$
$N$ Pareto efficiency on $Q>N$

Therefore, no alternative can be chosen without violating someone’s rights, or violating Pareto efficiency. Specifically, Clark vetoed the Pareto efficient alternative $Q$.

Formal Proof

Proof of Sen’s Theorem: Suppose we have two individuals, $C$ and $S$, and three alternatives, $T$, $Q$, and $N$. Assume we have a social welfare function that satisfies minimal liberalism, unrestricted domain, and transitivity. We will show that this social welfare function cannot satisfy Pareto efficiency.

Suppose that $C$ is decisive over $T$ and $Q$, and that $S$ is decisive over $T$ and $N$. Suppose that the preference orders are as follows:

Agent Preference Order
$C$ $T > Q > N$
$S$ $Q > N > T$

By $C$’s decisiveness over $T$ and $Q$, we have that if $C$ prefers $T>Q$, then we must have $T>Q$ in the social preference order. By $S$’s decisiveness over $T$ and $N$, we have that if $S$ prefers $N>T$, then we must have $N>T$ in the social preference order. Therefore, we have $N>T$ and $T>Q$ in the social preference order.

By transitivity, society must rank $N>Q$. However, Pareto efficiency requires that if both $C$ and $S$ prefer $Q>N$, then we must have $Q>N$ in the social preference order. Therefore, Pareto efficiency is violated. $\square$

The original example

His example was that of a Prude and a Lewd, and what they might decide to do with “Lady Chatterley’s Lover”. Essentially, they have to decide between

  • Nobody reads the book ($N$)
  • Only the Prude reads the book ($P$)
  • Only the Lewd reads the book ($L$)

The preference orders are as follows:

Person Preference Order
Prude $N > P > L$
Lewd $P > L > N$

Supposedly, the Prude wants neither of them to read the book. It’s filthy! But, if someone has to read it, it would be better that the Prude reads it (and is appropriately horrified by the contents) than the Lewd (who would be delighted by the contents).

The Lewd, on the other hand, for some reason, would rather the Prude read the book to “loosen up”. But if the Prude won’t read the book, then it’s better that the Lewd reads it than nobody reading it at all.

I do not like this example because these alternatives are not really mutually exclusive. Why can’t both read the book? Further, does it really make sense that the Lewd wants the Prude to read the book more than he wants to read it himself?

Sen’s theorem is controversial, but I won’t get into the general criticisms here. Perhaps in a future post. However, the intuitive idea that respecting individual rights, when they conflict with each other, can lead to suboptimal outcomes seems reasonable to me. I just don’t know if this theorem is as ground-shattering as Arrow’s or Gibbard’s theorems. But the proof is kind of neat, so there you go.

Hopefully you found the example that I gave to be somewhat intuitive!
