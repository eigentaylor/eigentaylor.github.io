Arrow's Theorem

Walkthrough and proof of the theorem that proves ranked voting systems cannot satisfy seemingly reasonable properties.

Introduction

I recently made a post on The Gibbard Satterthwaite Theorem, which shows that ranked voting systems cannot be strategy-proof. The Gibbard Satterthwaite Theorem is the cousin of the more famous Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem, which shows that ranked voting systems cannot satisfy a set of seemingly reasonable properties. In this post, I will give a walkthrough and proof of Arrow’s Theorem based on a very concise 1970 paper by the legendary Peter C Fishburn, who also shows that the theorem does not apply when we have infinitely many voters!

I also highly recommend this video by Veritasium, which gives a great introduction to the theorem and its implications. However, the clickbait title of “Why Democracy Is Mathematically Impossible” is a bit misleading. I would like to be up front about what I believe can and should be said about Arrow’s Theorem.

First off, and I feel most importantly: Arrow’s theorem does not imply that ranked voting systems are doomed. My (many) issues with ranked voting systems have nothing to do with Arrow’s Theorem or Gibbard-Satterthwaite. And the fact that Approval voting technically sidesteps both theorems is not even close to relevant to why I support Approval voting. However, I mention why Approval sidesteps these theorems purely because I find the proofs that they do to be very elegant. Approval voting is still fully bitten by Gibbard’s more general theorem.

Arrow’s theorem is a beautiful result, but it is not a death sentence for democracy. I might even call it a magic trick! Like the Gibbard-Satterthwaite Theorem, the underlying idea is “let’s suppose that a ranked voting system satisfies a seemingly innocent property, and then show that property is so restrictive it induces the need for a dictator.” For Gibbard-Satterthwaite, the property was strategyproofness. For Arrow’s Theorem, the killer property is the “independence of irrelevant alternatives” (IIA). Rather than start with a heavy definition, I’d like to explain the loose intuitive idea with a famous quote by Sidney Morgenbesser:

Morgenbesser, ordering dessert, is told by a waitress that he can choose between blueberry or apple pie. He orders apple. Soon the waitress comes back and explains cherry pie is also an option. Morgenbesser replies “In that case, I’ll have blueberry.”

The problem with IIA is that it’s misleadingly simple. It’s fairly easy to construct an example where this property is violated in our familiar choose-one voting system. For example, if we have two candidates, Alice and Bob, let’s suppose the election results are as follows:

Number of Voters 1st Choice 2nd Choice
61 Alice Bob
39 Bob Alice

Simple enough, Alice wins by quite a lot (61% of the vote). However, suppose Clark decides to run as a third candidate. Clark is very polarizing, and Bob supporters despise him. On the other hand, while many Alice supporters also find Clark detestable, most of them find Clark to be a very exciting candidate, and decide to support him instead of Alice. The new rankings as follows:

Number of Voters 1st Choice 2nd Choice 3rd Choice
28 Alice Bob Clark
33 Clark Alice Bob
39 Bob Alice Clark

Taking only the first choices into account, Bob gets first place with 39% of the vote, Clark comes in second with 33%, and Alice now comes in third with 28%. Unfortunately for Alice, Clark’s entry into the race has caused her to lose. Bob wins with 39% of the vote, while Alice only gets 28%. Not a single voter changed their mind about how they feel about Alice relative to Bob, but the mere presence of Clark has caused Alice to get fewer votes than Bob. We can also see that Clark had no real chance of winning, since 67% of voters preferred both Alice and Bob to Clark. But Clark was able to seed off enough votes from Alice to cause her to come in last place. Clark is the cherry pie that caused apple pie to lose to blueberry pie!

And, unfortunately, not even Ranked Choice Voting (RCV) can save Alice here, because she came in last place. By voting for Clark first, the Clark-first-Alice-second voters elected the candidate they liked least, Bob. This is the spoiler effect in action. 61% of voters still prefer Alice to Bob, but Bob wins because of the presence of Clark.

This is essentially what IIA says cannot happen. If we want to know how Alice compares to Bob, we should be able to look purely at how voters feel about Alice relative to Bob, and not have to worry about how voters feel about Clark. I like to call IIA a “stability” property. If we mess around with irrelevant alternatives, the standing of the relevant alternatives should stay “stable”.

The problem is, however, that IIA is extremely restrictive. Essentially no “reasonable” ranked voting system can satisfy IIA. The typical proof of Arrow’s Theorem typically shows that if you have a “responsive” voting system that satisfies IIA, then it can only be responsive to one voter, and that voter is a dictator. Fishburn’s proof actually goes a different route, and shows that if you have a “responsive”, “stable” (IIA) voting system that isn’t a dictatorship, then you must have infinitely many voters. Which is too mathematically cute for me not to write a blog post about!

Preliminaries

We imagine that we have a set of voters $V$, a set of candidates $X$ with at least 3 candidates, and a voting system $\sigma$ that takes in “weak orders” (rankings of candidates that allow for ties) from each voter (denoted as a function like $f$ that takes in a voter $f(v)$ and outputs their ranking) and outputs a “weak order” of the candidates.

One key requirement is that we should allow any possible ranking of the candidates to be submitted by the voters, and the voting system should be able to spit out a ranking of the candidates. So just because I submit a wacky contrarian ballot shouldn’t cause the voting system to catch on fire and cancel the election. To be a weak order, we also need a basic sense of transitivity. If I rank Bob $\geq$ Alice and Clark $\geq$ Bob, then I should also rank Clark $\geq$ Alice.

We need at least three candidates to have a non-trivial voting system. With only two candidates, we can just pick the candidate with the most votes, and that voting system is perfectly fine and satisfies any reasonable property we could want. With three or more candidates, things get interesting.

Our voting system $\sigma$ is a function that takes in the voters’ preferences and outputs a collective preference. Essentially, you can think of this as taking in everyone’s ranked ballots and outputting a single ranked ballot that represents the collective preference of the voters. So we can ask “how does society rank Alice relative to Bob?” by looking at the output of $\sigma$.

Now here we come with the three, less technical properties that we want our voting system to satisfy:

  1. Unanimity: If every voter prefers candidate $A$ to candidate $B$, then society should also prefer $A$ to $B$.
  2. Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA): The voting system’s ranking of $A$ relative to $B$ should only depend on how voters rank $A$ relative to $B$, and not on how voters rank other candidates.
  3. Non-Dictatorship: There should not be a single voter who always gets their way, regardless of the preferences of the other voters.

Now, the typical proof shows that a unanimous voting system satisfying IIA must be a dictatorship. Fishburn’s proof takes a different route and shows that if we have a unanimous voting system satisfying IIA that is not a dictatorship, then we must have infinitely many voters. This is a very elegant way to show that the only way to satisfy these properties is to have an infinite number of voters, which is not practical in the real world.

Now the above explanations are the very loose intuitive ideas behind these properties. The actual definitions are a bit more technical, but it’s going to help to define our notation. I personally found the notation used in Fishburn’s paper to be a bit… dense. So I will be defining my own notation, more consistent with my proof of Gibbard-Satterthwaite, which I hope is a bit more intuitive.

Notation

We denote that voter $v$ ranks $a>b$ as \(a >_{v} b\). For a subset of voters $U$, we denote \(a >_U b\) to mean that every voter in $U$ ranks $a>b$. Our notation for a single voter is exactly the same as our notation for a subset of voters. To be clear about if $v$ or $U$ ranks $a>b$ in a particular profile $f$, then we might denote it $a >{v_f} b$ or $a >{U_f} b$.

If voter $v$ (or subset $U$) does not rank \(a>_v b\), we denote this as \(b\geq_v a\). This means either that voter $v$ ranks $b>a$ or that voter $v$ ranks them equally.

If the profile in use is clear from context, we denote that the voting system $\sigma$ ranks $a>b$ as \(a >_\sigma b\). This means that the output of the voting system ranks $a$ above $b$. We can also write \(a \geq_\sigma b\) to mean that the voting system ranks $a$ above or equal to $b$. If the profile needs specification, we will use \(\sigma(f)\) or \(\sigma(g)\) to be clear about which profile we’re talking about.

We say that two profiles $f$ and $g$ “agree” or are “equal” on ${a,b}$ if every voters relative ranking of $a$ and $b$ are the same in both profiles. That is, \(a >_v b\) in $f$ if and only if \(a >_v b\) in $g$ for every voter $v$.

We’re often concerned with a single voter at a time, $v$. We denote the set of every voter besides $v$ ($V-{v}$) as $V’$.

We also define a sense of a blocking set. If society ranks $a>b$ whenever $U$ ranks $a>b$, then we say that $U$ “blocks” $b$ with $a$: denoted \(a\triangleright_U b\). In particular, \(a\triangleright_{V'}b\) means that if every voter besides $v$ ranks $a>b$, then society ranks $a>b$, even if $v$ ranks $b>a$.

The Proof

Lemma: There is more than one voter in $V$.

Proof: If there was only one voter in $V$, they would be a dictator. Therefore, we must have more than one voter: \(\|V\|>1\). QED

Proposition: Any single voter can be overruled by all other voters in their preference of any two candidates. That is, for any voter $v$ and any distinct candidates $a$ and $b$, we have \(a\triangleright_{V'} b\) or \(b\triangleright_{V'} a\).\label{singleblock}

We will denote this property as \(\triangleright_1\). We will prove this incrementally.

Lemma: (Overruling lemma) If \(p\triangleright_{V'}q\), then for any $r$ distinct from $p,q$,\label{lem:overrule}

  • (i) \(p\triangleright_{V'}r\)
  • (ii) \(r\triangleright_{V'}q\)

Proof: Consider the following profile $f_1$:

  • $v$ ranks: $q>r>p$
  • all voters in $V’$ rank: $r>p>q$

By the assumption that \(p\triangleright_{V'}q\), we must have that society ranks $p>q$, since $V’$ ranks $p>q$. By unanimity, every voter ranks $r>p$, so society must rank $r>p$. By transitivity, society ranks $r>q$. Thus, we have that $v$ ranks $q>r$ but $V’$ ranks $r>q$, and society ranks $r>q$. Therefore, we have \(r\triangleright_{V'}q\). That is, we showed that $v$ can be overruled on $q$ over $r$.

Consider the profile $f_2$:

  • $v$ ranks: $q>r>p$
  • all voters in $V’$ rank: $p>q>r$

Similarly, by the assumption that \(p\triangleright_{V'}q\), we must have that society ranks $p>q$, since $V’$ ranks $p>q$. By unanimity, every voter ranks $q>r$, so society must rank $q>r$. By transitivity, society ranks $p>r$. Thus, we have that $v$ ranks $r>p$ but $V’$ ranks $p>r$, and society ranks $p>r$. Therefore, we have \(p\triangleright_{V'}r\). That is, we showed that $v$ can be overruled on $p$ over $r$. QED

This means that if $v$ can be overruled on $p$ over $q$, then $v$ can also be overruled on $p$ over any other candidate, and on any other candidate over $q$.

Lemma: For any single voter $v$ who ranks \(a>_v b\), there exists a candidate \(x\) such that \(b\triangleright_{V'} x\).\label{singlex}

Proof: Since $v$ cannot be a dictator, by assumption. We must have some profile $f$ where $v$ ranks $a>b$ but society ranks \(b\geq a\).

We construct a new profile $g$ where everyone has the same relative ranking of $a$ and $b$ as in $f$, but we have

  1. $v$ ranks $a>x>b$ for some distinct candidate $x$.
  2. Every other voter ranks $b>x$ and $a>x$.

Basically, we take the profile $f$ where $v$ is overruled on $a$ and $b$, and we insert a new candidate $x$ in between $a$ and $b$ for voter $v$, and insert $x$ at the bottom of the ranking for every other voter. This new profile $g$ agrees with $f$ on ${a,b}$, so by IIA, we must have \(b \geq a\) in $g$ as well.

By unanimity, society must rank $a$ over $x$. This means society ranks $a>x$ and $b\geq a$, so by transitivity, society ranks $b > x$. Thus, we have that $v$ ranks $x>b$ but $V’$ ranks $b>x$, and society ranks $b>x$. Therefore, we have \(b\triangleright_{V'} x\). QED

Now we can prove proposition \ref{singleblock}

Proof: Let $v$ be any voter and $a,b$ be any distinct candidates. We want to show that \(a\triangleright_{V'} b\) and \(b\triangleright_{V'} a\). By Lemma \ref{singlex}, we have that \(b\triangleright_{V'} x\). We apply the Overruling Lemma repeatedly.

  • \(b\triangleright_{V'} x\) directly we obtain \(b\triangleright_{V'} a\) by the Overruling Lemma (i).
  • \(b\triangleright_{V'} x\) implies \(y\triangleright_{V'} x\), by the Overruling Lemma (ii), for any candidate $y$ distinct from $b$ and $x$. In particular, we have \(a\triangleright_{V'} x\).
  • By the Overruling Lemma (i), we have \(a\triangleright_{V'} b\). QED

Therefore, we have shown that any single voter $v$ can be overruled by all other voters in their preference of any two candidates. This is a whole lot of rigorous math to show the most basic assumption I think anyone would expect from literally any voting system: that if every voter except one prefers $a$ to $b$, then society should also prefer $a$ to $b$.

A perceptive reader may notice something odd, though. What if there are only two voters? In that case, we get a strange asymmetry, because if $v$ can be overruled by… one other voter, then that other voter can also be overruled by $v$. However, we never said how many voters there are. The next step is to show that if $n$ voters can be overruled, at all, then,

  1. We must have more than $n+1$ voters, and
  2. $n+1$ voters can be overruled.

We will do this with a trick called “mathematical induction”. I know some non-mathematicians read this blog, so I want to make sure that we’re all on the same page about what mathematical induction is. However, if you are a proud mathematician who does induction proofs for breakfast, you can skip to the next section.

A brief introduction to mathematical induction

The idea is quite simple, and I will borrow the explanation of one of the greatest professors I’ve ever known: If you want to climb an infinite ladder, you need only two things.

  1. You need to be able to get on the first rung of the ladder.
  2. You need to be able to get from any rung to the next rung.

If you can do those two things, you can climb as high as you want! You can get on the first rung, and then you can get to the second rung, and then you can get to the third rung, and so on. You can climb as high as you want, even though there are infinitely many rungs.

Therefore, we have shown a single voter can be overruled. Now, if we can show that $n$ voters can be overruled implies that $n+1$ voters can be overruled (and that we have more than $n+1$ voters), then we can conclude that any number of voters can be overruled, which means we must have infinitely many voters. This is the essence of mathematical induction.

Infinitely Many Voters

Proposition: If any strict subset of size $n$ voters can be overruled (implying $V$ contains more than $n$ voters), then $V$ must have more than $n+1$ voters, and $n+1$ voters can be overruled. \label{inductionstep}

If you read my Gibbard-Satterthwaite post, this proof construction might seem familiar! However, the precise mechanism is slightly different, as we shall see.

Proof: Let $U$ be a subset of $n$ voters, and that $V$ contains more than $n$ voters. That is, $U$ is a strict subset of $V$. And suppose that $n$ voters can always be overruled. Then let $v$ be a voter outside of $U$, and denote $S’=V-(U\cup{v})$. Meaning $S’$ is all voters besides those in $U$ and $v$. We allow the possibility that we have only $n+1$ voters and $S’$ is empty. Consider the following profile:

  • $v$ ranks: $a>b>c$
  • Every voter in $U$ ranks: $b>c>a$
  • Every voter in $S’$ ranks: $c>a>b$

We know that any single voter can be overruled by Proposition \ref{singleblock}, and we have that every voter besides $v$ ranks $c>a$. Therefore, society must rank $c>a$. Similarly, by assumption that a strict subset of size $n$ can be overruled, we have that $U$ ranks $b>a$, but everyone outside of $U$ ranks $a>b$. Thus, society must rank $a>b$. By transititivity, we must have that society ranks $c>b$.

If $S’$ is empty, then we would only have voters $v$ and those in $U$. So, by unanimity, we would need that society ranks $b>c$, which would be a contradiction. We must have that $S’$ is nonempty, so $V$ must contain strictly more than $n+1$ voters. Therefore, the set of size $n+1$, $U\cup{v}$, can be overruled. Since $U,v,a,b,c$ were arbitrary, we have sufficiently shown that any set of size $n+1$ can be overruled in the decision of any two candidates. QED

Theorem: (Arrow’s Theorem) If $\sigma$ is a voting system that satisfies unanimity, IIA, and contains no dictators, then it must be defined on a set of infinitely many voters.

This is certainly not the regular formulation of Arrow’s theorem, but it is equivalent, which we will show directly after the proof:

Proof: Proof by induction. Lemma \ref{morethanone} tells us that we must have more than one voter. Proposition \ref{singleblock} tells us that any single voter can be overruled. Proposition \ref{inductionstep} tells us that if a strict subset of $n$ voters can be overruled, then $V$ must contain more than $n+1$ voters, and that $n+1$ voters can be overruled. Therefore, $V$ cannot contain any finite number of voters. QED

To ensure the logic is clear, we had the proposition that a single voter can be overruled. By the inductive proposition, this tells us that we must have more than $1+1=2$ voters, and that $2$ voters can be overruled. Applying the inductive proposition again tells us we must have more than $3$ voters and that $3$ voters can be overruled. And so on, and so on. If you ask me “what about a set of a bajillion-gooleplex voters?”, then it would take… a while… but I could go from the fact that one voter can be blocked to show that… that many voters can be blocked and we must have strictly more than that number of voters.

Normally, Arrow’s theorem is written as the following:

  1. $V$ has a finite number of voters.
  2. We have more than $3$ candidates to choose from.
  3. We have a voting system that can take any weak orders in and output a weak ordering of candidates.
  4. The voting system is unanimous: if every voter ranks $a>b$, then society ranks $a>b$.
  5. IIA: changing the ordering of irrelevant alternatives cannot change the relative ordering of the relevant alternatives.
  6. No dictators: no single voter can decide the social ordering entirely.

Arrow’s theorem typically says that if you satisfy the first 5 conditions, then you must violate the 6th condition. That is, you must have a dictator. However, we actually proved that if you have conditions 2 through 6, then the first condition must be violated! Which I think is just the neatest darn thing I ever did see.

Fishburn then also shows that if you have infinitely many voters, then you can actually define a sort of coherent voting system on this infinite set of voters using something called a “probability measure”. Now, I won’t start getting into measure theory, but I do think I can sufficiently explain the concept intuitively. But it would, I think, be helpful first to take a detour to a more commonly discussed type of restriction on voting systems: the one-dimensional single peaked preference restriction.

Single Peaked Preferences

I think the comparison is helpful here. If we suppose that all candidates and voters lie on a one-dimensional axis (ex. left for liberal and right for conservative), and have voters rank candidates based on who is closest to them, then we get a restriction on the possible ranked preferences that voters are able to make.

For example, it’s impossible for any voter to rank a middle candidate last. This means that Arrow’s theorem does not apply, since we are restricted in what kinds of rankings voters are able to express. But this imposed restriction gives us something important: transitivity.

If we define a voting system on this unidimensional axis by simply taking majority rule in each pairwise match-up, then this is a fully coherent voting system. I am simplifying this excessively, as this is merely an illustrative example. This is actually a much more general concept, but for our purposes, we will define that a voter ranks candidates by their exact (Euclidean) distance.

Theorem: If voters have single peaked preferences, and we denote $m$ as the location of the median voter’s preference, then society ranks \(a\geq b\) if and only if \(dist(m,a)\leq dist(m,b)\). Where \(dist(m,a)\) is the distance between $m$ and the position of candidate $x$.

Proof: A voter $v$ will rank $a>b$ if and only if $dist(v,a)<dist(v,b)$. Therefore, the decision to rank $a>b$ depends on a voter’s position relative to the midpoint between $a$ and $b$: \(\frac{a+b}{2}=\mu_{ab}\). If we assume, without loss of generality, that $a$ is positioned to the left of $b$, then

\[a>_v b \iff dist(v,a)<dist(v,b) \iff v<\mu_{ab}\]

Suppose the median voter is positioned $m<\mu_{ab}$. Then we have that at least half of voters are positioned to the left of $\mu_{ab}$, so at least half of voters rank $a>b$. By majority rule, this means $m<\mu_{ab}$ implies that society must rank $a\geq b$. Similarly, if the median voter is to the right of the endpoint, then society will rank $b\geq a$. If the median voter is positioned precisely at the midpoint, $m=\mu_{ab}$, then that implies that $a$ and $b$ will be tied in the societal ranking. Thus,

\[a\geq_{\sigma} b\iff m\leq \frac{a+b}{2}\iff dist(m,a)\leq dist(m,b)\]

QED

Transitivity comes directly from this:

Theorem: The societal ranking of candidates under unidimensional single peaked preferences is transitive.

Proof: Suppose that society ranks \(a\geq b\) and \(b\geq c\). Then, $dist(m,a)\leq dist(m,b)$ and $dist(m,b)\leq dist(m,c)$$. By transitivity of real numbers, $dist(m,a)\leq dist(m,c)$ implies society ranks $a\geq c$. QED

The point of this example is to illustrate that if we induce certain restrictions on how voters can rank things, then the societal ranking becomes strictly coherent.
