## Introduction

I recently made a post on [The Gibbard Satterthwaite Theorem](../gibbard-satt){:target="_blank"}, which shows that ranked voting systems cannot be strategyproof. The Gibbard Satterthwaite Theorem is the cousin of the more famous [Arrow's Impossibility Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow%27s_impossibility_theorem), which shows that ranked voting systems cannot satisfy a set of seemingly reasonable properties. In this post, I will give a walkthrough and proof of Arrow's Theorem based on [a very concise 1970 paper by the legendary Peter C Fishburn](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0022053170900153).

Kenneth Arrow received his PhD in 1951 and [co-won a Nobel Prize in Economics in 1972 for "pioneering contributions to general equilibrium theory and welfare theory."](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/bios/Arrow.html), and is primarily known for his impossibility theorem.

I also highly recommend [this video by Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qf7ws2DF-zk), which gives a great introduction to the theorem and its implications. However, the clickbait title of "Why Democracy Is Mathematically Impossible" is a bit misleading, though I respect the grind. I would like to be up front about what I believe can and should be said about Arrow's Theorem.

[Arrow himself has said:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow's_impossibility_theorem#Interpretation_and_practical_solutions)

> "Most systems are not going to work badly all of the time. All I proved is that all can work badly at times." (Kenneth Arrow)

Arrow's theorem is a *beautiful* result, but it is not a death sentence for democracy, or even, more specifically, ranked voting systems. I might even call it a magic trick! Like the Gibbard-Satterthwaite Theorem, the underlying idea is as follows:

- In my hand, I hold an innocent, innocuous property. Examine the perfect voting system which would satisfy such a property. There is nothing up my sleeves!
- From this property, I derive constraints on how the voting system must behave. Step by step, these constraints become increasingly restrictive.
- Behold, as out of my hat, I pull a dictator!

<img src="/assets/img/magician_dictator.png" alt="A magician pulls a dictator out of a hat" style="width:100%; max-width:600px;">

A dictator is a much more concerning thing to pull out of a hat than a rabbit. But that's generally how these proofs go. The proof I am going to present does it a bit differently, however.

For Gibbard-Satterthwaite, the property was strategyproofness. For Arrow's Theorem, the killer property is the "independence of irrelevant alternatives" (IIA). Rather than start with a heavy definition, I'd like to explain the loose intuitive idea with a [famous joke about Sidney Morgenbesser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_irrelevant_alternatives#Motivation):

> Morgenbesser, ordering dessert, is told by a waitress that he can choose between blueberry or apple pie. He orders apple. Soon the waitress comes back and explains cherry pie is also an option. Morgenbesser replies "In that case, I'll have blueberry."

At the risk of ruining the joke by explaining it, the humor comes from the fact that Morgenbesser clearly prefers apple to blueberry. Therefore, it would make little sense for him to ever pick blueberry if apple is on the menu. How could the presence of cherry pie cause him to change his mind about apple vs. blueberry?

### The Pie Problem

At the individual level, it's easy enough to maintain coherent preference orders. However, the key takeaway from Arrow's theorem is that it becomes essentially impossible to maintain such rationality when we start aggregating multiple coherent individual preferences together. While Morgenbesser would never do this consciously on his own, let us see how leaving the decision of dessert up to a group of rational dinner guests can lead to the same kind of irrationality that Morgenbesser's joke illustrates. Let us say the 100 guests at the dinner party are split in two groups as follows:

| Number of Voters | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice |
|------------------|------------|------------|
| 61               | Apple      | Blueberry  |
| 39               | Blueberry  | Apple      |

Simple enough, Apple wins by quite a lot (61% of the vote). However, suppose now the waitress gives our guests the option to pick Cherry. Cherry is very polarizing, and Blueberry supporters despise it. On the other hand, while many Apple supporters also find Cherry detestable, most of them find Cherry to be a very exciting and delectable dish, and decide to support it instead of Apple, despite still preferring Apple to Blueberry. The new rankings are as follows:

| Number of Voters | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice | 3rd Choice |
|------------------|------------|------------|------------|
| 30               | Apple      | Blueberry  | Cherry     |
| 31               | Cherry     | Apple      | Blueberry  |
| 39               | Blueberry  | Apple      | Cherry     |

Taking only the first choices into account, Blueberry gets first place with 39% of the vote, Cherry comes in second with 31%, and Apple now comes in third with 30%. Unfortunately for Apple, Cherry's entry into the race has caused it to lose. Blueberry wins with 39% of the vote, while Apple only gets 30%. Not a single voter changed their mind about how they feel about Apple relative to Blueberry, but the mere presence of Cherry has caused Apple to get fewer votes than Blueberry. We can also see that Cherry had no real chance of winning, since 69% of voters (over two thirds) preferred both Apple and Blueberry to Cherry. But Cherry was able to seed off enough votes from Apple to cause it to come in last place.

And, unfortunately, not even Ranked Choice Voting (RCV) can save Apple here, because it came in last place. Apple, the only candidate who could defeat Blueberry, will be eliminated first, and Cherry will lose in the final round to Blueberry. By voting for Cherry first, the Cherry-first-Apple-second voters effectively ordered the dessert they liked least, Blueberry. This is the spoiler effect in action. That majority of 61% of voters *still* prefer Apple to Blueberry, but Blueberry wins because of the presence of Cherry.

And, worst of all, if just one of the Cherry-first voters had instead ranked Apple first and Cherry second, Apple would have survived the first round and ultimately defeated Blueberry in the final round, despite no voter changing their relative preference of Apple and Blueberry. Let that sink in: this voter still ranks Blueberry below both Apple and Cherry. But the preference between Apple and Cherry decides if Blueberry defeats Apple.

This is not some abstract or theoretical violation either. This has happened in real Ranked-Choice voting elections! [Alaska's 2022 Special Election](https://ranked.vote/report/us/ak/2022/08/cd) and [Burlington's 2009 Mayoral Election](https://ranked.vote/report/us/vt/btv/2009/03/mayor) had similar dynamics where spoiler candidates caused a consensus option, who would have defeated all other candidates, to be eliminated first.

This is essentially what IIA says should not happen. If we want to know how Apple compares to Blueberry, we should be able to look purely at how voters feel about Apple relative to Blueberry, and not have to worry about how voters feel about Cherry. I like to call IIA a "stability" property. If we mess around with irrelevant candidates, the standing and order of the relevant alternatives that we didn't adjust should stay "stable", in the same order.

The problem is, however, that IIA is *extremely* restrictive. Essentially no "reasonable" *ranked* voting system can satisfy IIA. The typical proof of Arrow's Theorem shows that if you have a "responsive" voting system that satisfies IIA, then it makes the system so rigid that it can only be responsive to one voter, and that voter is a dictator. Fishburn's proof actually goes a different route, and shows that if you have a "responsive", "stable" (IIA) voting system that *isn't* a dictatorship, then you must have *infinitely many voters*. Which is too mathematically cute for me *not* to write a blog post about!

## Preliminaries

In total, Fishburn identifies six total properties that Arrow's theorem concerns itself with. Loosely, they are as follows:

1. Having a finite number of voters
2. Having three or more candidates
3. Allowing any possible input ranking of candidates (with ties allowed) from all voters as input, and outputting a societal ordering of candidates (with ties allowed). This is called "unrestricted domain".
4. If all voters agree and rank $a>b$, then society should also rank $a>b$ (unanimity)
5. If every voter maintains their relative ranking of $a$ and $b$, then society should maintain the same relative ranking of $a$ and $b$, even if we move around any or all other candidates (IIA)
6. No single voter decides how society ranks one candidate over another. This would be a "dictator".

> **Theorem:** (Arrow's Theorem) The above 6 properties cannot be satisfied simultaneously by any ranked voting system.

The typical proof of Arrow's theorem shows that if you satisfy the first five conditions, then the sixth has to be violated. The essential idea being that IIA requires the system to be so rigid that it can only be responsive to a single voter. Fishburn instead shows that conditions 2 through 6 imply that the first is impossible: If you want to satisfy all those nice properties, you need an infinite number of voters.

I'd like to really make sure the fundamental ideas of these concepts are rock solid, so forgive me for giving them special attention.

We imagine that we have a set of voters $V$, a set of at least 3 candidates, and a voting system $\sigma$ that takes in "weak orders" (rankings of candidates that allow for ties) from each voter and outputs a "weak order" of the candidates, which we call the "societal ranking".

One key requirement is that we should allow any possible "ranking" of the candidates to be submitted by the voters, and the voting system should be able to spit out a ranking of the candidates. So just because I submit a wacky contrarian ballot shouldn't cause the voting machine to catch on fire and cancel the election.

We won't get into the super technical jargon of what it means to be a coherent "ranking with ties", or a "weak ordering". We will just call this a "ranking", which is exactly what you imagine a ranking with ties to be. For that, we need a basic sense of *transitivity*. If I rank Blueberry $\geq$ Apple and Cherry $\geq$ Blueberry, then I should also rank Cherry $\geq$ Apple. A careful reader may nitpick this is technically "negative transitivity", but I do not think anything is lost by simply calling this "transitivity", and a weak ordering a "ranking" (with ties).

We need at least three candidates to have a non-trivial voting system. With only two candidates, we can just pick the candidate with the most votes, and that voting system is perfectly fine and satisfies any reasonable property we could want. With three or more candidates, things get interesting, and start breaking.

Our voting system $\sigma$ is a function that takes in the voters' preferences and outputs a collective preference. Essentially, you can think of this as taking in everyone's ranked ballots and outputting a single ranked ballot that represents the collective preference of the voters. So we can ask "how does society rank Apple relative to Blueberry?" by looking at the output of $\sigma$, and comparing if Apple is ranked above Blueberry in the societal ranking.

Now here we come with the three, more technical properties that we want our voting system to satisfy:

1. **Unanimity**: If every voter prefers candidate $a$ to candidate $b$, then society should also prefer $a$ to $b$. This is also sometimes called "Pareto efficiency".
2. **Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA)**: If we move from one profile to another profile, and every voter maintains their relative ranking of $a$ and $b$, then society should also maintain the same relative ranking of $a$ and $b$, even if we move around any or all other candidates.
3. **Non-Dictatorship**: There should not be a single voter such that whenever they rank $a>b$, then so does society. More intuitively, the final result should not just be a single voter's ballot, regardless of what every other voter does.

Now, I personally found the notation used in Fishburn's paper to be a bit... dense. So I will be defining my own notation, [more consistent with my proof of Gibbard-Satterthwaite](../gibbard-satt){:target="_blank"}, which I hope is a bit more intuitive.

### Notation

We denote that voter $v$ ranks $a>b$ as $a >_{v} b$.

If the profile in use is clear from context, we denote that the voting system $\sigma$ ranks $a>b$ as $$a >_{\sigma} b$$. This means that the output of the voting system ranks $a$ above $b$. We can also write $$a \geq_{\sigma} b$$ to mean that the voting system ranks $a$ above or equal to $b$.

We say that two profiles "agree" or are "equal" on $\{a,b\}$ if every voter's relative ranking of $a$ and $b$ are the same in both profiles. That is, $a >_v b$ in one profile if and only if $a >_v b$ in the other profile for every voter $v$. So if we have one profile where a voter ranks $a>b>c$ and then another where they rank $a>c>b$, then those two profiles agree on $\{a,b\}$, because the voter ranks $a>b$ in both profiles. This is important for IIA, because if two profiles agree on $\{a,b\}$, then IIA requires that the societal ranking of $a$ versus $b$ be the same in both profiles, regardless of how the other candidates are ranked.

However, if we have a profile where the voter ranks $b>a>c$, then that profile does not agree with the first two profiles on $\{a,b\}$, because the voter ranks $b>a$ in that profile. Then the societal ranking of $a$ versus $b$ might change when moving from one profile to another.

We're often concerned with a single voter at a time, $v$. We denote the set of every voter besides $v$ ($V-\{v\}$) as $V'$.

## The Proof

Note: This proof is loosely based on Fishburn's proof, but I have made some adjustments to the structure, notation, terminology, and filled in some details to make it more accessible. Fishburn's proof is concise and elegant, but it's a bit hard to follow. Lemma \ref{overrule} is not in Fishburn's paper, but it makes the proof of Proposition \ref{single-block} much more intuitive and easier to follow, in my opinion.

For the proof, we assume that we have an "Arrovian voting function" $\sigma$ on $V$ that satisfies properties 2 through 6. That is,

> **Definition:** We call a voting function $\sigma$ an "Arrovian" voting function on $V$ if $\sigma$ is a voting system that takes in any possible profile of voters' ranked preferences and outputs a societal ranking of three or more candidates, satisfies unanimity and IIA, and for which no single voter in $V$ is a dictator.

This is essentially our ideal "fair" voting system. To show that the 6 properties Arrow and Fishburn define cannot be satisfied simultaneously, we will prove that if $\sigma$ is Arrovian on $V$, then $V$ must have infinitely many voters.

> **Lemma:** If $\sigma$ is Arrovian on $V$, then there is more than one voter in $V$.\label{more-than-one}

**Proof:** If there was only one voter in $V$, they would be a dictator. Therefore, $V$ must have more than one voter. $\square$

> **Definition:** We say that a voter $v$ can be overruled with $a$ over $b$ if whenever every voter except $v$ ranks $a>b$, then society ranks $a>b$, even if $v$ ranks $b>a$. In other words, $v$'s vote cannot flip the $a$ vs. $b$ outcome against the unanimous preference of every other voter. We denote this property $avb$, and say that "$v$ is overruled on $a$ over $b$".

By IIA, it's sufficient to show that there exists a single "critical" profile where $v$ ranks $b>a$ and both $V'$ and society rank $a>b$. If such a critical profile exists, then no matter how anyone ranks any other candidate in any other profile where $v$ ranks $b>a$ and everyone in $V'$ ranks $a>b$, IIA would guarantee the same societal relative ranking of $a>b$ as this critical profile, since they would have to agree on the relative rankings of $a$ and $b$.

> **Proposition:** In an Arrovian voting system, any single voter can be overruled by all other voters in their preference of any two candidates. That is, for any voter $v$ and any distinct candidates $a$ and $b$, we have $avb$ and $bva$.\label{single-block}

We will prove this incrementally.

> **Lemma:** (Overruling lemma) If $pvq$ in an Arrovian voting system, then for any $r$ distinct from $p,q$,\label{overrule}
>
> - (i) $rvq$
> - (ii) $pvr$

\proofref{overrule} in appendix.

Basically, if $v$ can be overruled with $p$ over $q$, then $v$ can also be overruled with $p$ over any other candidate, and any other candidate over $q$. In other words, the ability of the rest of the electorate to overrule a single voter on a particular pair of candidates "propagates" to any other candidate when paired with either $p$ or $q$.

This is one of the most important steps of the proof, so let's try to ground the intuitive idea, as I think that's more illuminating than reading the formal proof. Suppose we have a voter Victor, who is a bit of a contrarian. Victor likes to go against the grain: if everyone else prefers Pete to Quincy, Victor will prefer Quincy to Pete.

And we assume that Victor can be overruled in this preference: if all the other voters prefer Pete to Quincy, then society will rank Pete over Quincy even if Victor prefers Quincy to Pete. And let's say this is the baseline scenario: Victor is being overruled on Pete versus Quincy by the *entire electorate* who all agree that Pete should be elected over Quincy.

| Group   | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice |
|---------|------------|------------|
| Victor  | Quincy     | Pete       |
| Rest    | Pete       | Quincy     |
| Society | Pete       | Quincy     |

Now, suppose that there is a third candidate, Rachel, who enters the race. Victor still likes Quincy the most, but prefers Rachel to Pete. The rest of the electorate, still prefers Pete to Quincy, but *loves* Rachel. How does this affect the societal ranking?

| Voters | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice | 3rd Choice |
|--------|------------|------------|------------|
| Victor | Quincy     | Rachel     | Pete       |
| Rest   | Rachel     | Pete       | Quincy     |

In this scenario, everyone agrees that Rachel is preferred to Pete, so society must rank Rachel over Pete. It would be silly to do anything else!

But the baseline was that Pete must be preferred over Quincy, since that's what everyone but Victor says. Thus, if society ranks Rachel over Pete and Pete over Quincy, by transitivity society must rank Rachel over Quincy. This means that society's ranking is *exactly* the same ranking as what everyone besides Victor prefers: Rachel over Pete over Quincy.

Observe what we have now:

- Victor prefers Quincy over Rachel
- The rest of the electorate prefers Rachel over Quincy
- Society ranks Rachel over Quincy

Victor is thus overruled on the pair Quincy versus Rachel in this profile.

Now, by IIA, we know that whenever Victor ranks Quincy over Rachel while everyone else ranks Rachel over Quincy, society must still rank Rachel over Quincy.

Therefore, Victor can be overruled by the rest of the electorate on the pair Quincy versus Rachel in *all profiles*. And that's the fundamental idea behind the proof of the lemma.

As an exercise to to ensure your understanding, see if you can maybe logic through the reasoning that Victor can be overruled by the rest of the electorate on Pete versus Rachel if everyone else besides Victor ranks Pete > Quincy > Rachel.

This tells us that being overruled on just one pair of candidates implies they can be overruled with many other pairs. But the key to this result is that to be Arrovian we need unanimity, independence, and transitivity. The transitivity, in particular, makes this overruling property so strong.

> **Lemma:** For any single voter $v$ who ranks $a>_v b$ in an Arrovian voting system, there exists a candidate $r$ such that $bvr$.\label{single-r}

\proofref{single-r} in appendix.

Similar to the above proof, we use *very* similar logic. However, rather than the *entire* electorate being in total agreement, here we only need something weaker. Let's walk through the logic carefully, as we did above.

We assume that our favorite little contrarian, Victor, is still not getting his way. Victor isn't a dictator, so even if he prefers Alice to Bob, there must be *some* way that Bob doesn't lose to Alice. That is, Bob is ranked equally or higher than Alice in society's ranking (Bob $\geq$ Alice). Now let's say Rachel, like last time, jumps into the race! Victor, like last time, puts Rachel in the middle of his ranking, under Alice but above Bob, while every other voter *hates* Rachel. They put Rachel dead last in their rankings. Poor Rachel.

IIA tells us that since society ranked Bob $\geq$ Alice before, the introduction of Rachel shouldn't change that. So even with Rachel in the race, society must maintain that preference.

| Group    | Preferences                  |
|----------|------------------------------|
| Victor   | Alice > Rachel > Bob         |
| Others   | Alice > Rachel, Bob > Rachel |
| Society  | Bob $\geq$ Alice             |

We don't know how the others rank Alice or Bob. All we know is that every voter ranks Rachel last, and society at least ranks Bob $\geq$ Alice.

However, notice that everyone agrees that Alice is better than Rachel, so Alice has to defeat Rachel. But we assumed that Bob is ranked *at least as high as* Alice in society's ranking. So society must rank Bob $\geq$ Alice > Rachel, and thus Bob strictly defeats Rachel.

This means that Victor, once again, is a contrarian when it comes to Rachel and Bob. Everyone but Victor agrees Bob is better than Rachel, and society agrees! Thus, Victor has been overruled on Bob versus Rachel.

In summary, by just knowing that Victor didn't get his way on Alice versus Bob, we were able to show that Victor can be overruled on Bob versus some other candidate, who in this case is named Rachel. This is the start of our chain of overrulings, which we will use to show that Victor can be overruled on *any* pair of candidates.

Now we can prove Proposition \ref{single-block}.

> **Proposition \ref{single-block}:** In an Arrovian voting system, any single voter can be overruled by all other voters in their preference of any two candidates. That is, for any voter $v$ and any distinct candidates $a$ and $b$, we have $avb$ and $bva$.

**Proof:** Let $v$ be any voter and $a,b$ be any distinct candidates. We want to show that $avb$ and $bva$. By Lemma \ref{single-r}, we have that $bvr$. We apply the Overruling Lemma repeatedly.

- $bvr$ directly gives us $avr$, by the Overruling Lemma (i).
- $bvr$ implies $bva$ by the Overruling Lemma (ii).
- By the Overruling Lemma (ii) applied to $avr$, we have $avb$. $\square$

Therefore, we have shown that any single voter $v$ can be overruled by all other voters in their preference of any two candidates. This is a whole lot of rigorous math to show the most basic assumption I think anyone would expect from literally any voting system with at least three voters: that if *every* voter except *one* prefers $a$ to $b$, then society should also prefer $a$ to $b$.

A perceptive reader may notice something odd, though. What if there are only two voters? We only said we have more than one. In that case, we *would* get a strange asymmetry, because if Victor can be overruled by... one other voter, then that other voter can also be overruled by Victor. However, we never said how many voters there are! We will show that we can't have two voters. We can't even have three. We need many, many more.

The next step of the proof is to show that if $n$ voters can be overruled, at all, then,

1. We must have more than $n+1$ voters, and
2. $n+1$ voters can be overruled.

We will do this with a trick called "mathematical induction". I know some non-mathematicians read this blog, so I want to make sure that we're all on the same page about what mathematical induction is. However, if you are a proud mathematician who does induction proofs for breakfast, you can [skip to the next section](#infinitely-many-voters).

### A brief introduction to mathematical induction

The idea is quite simple, and I will borrow the explanation of one of the greatest professors I've ever known: If you want to climb an infinite ladder, you need only two things.

1. You need to be able to get on the first rung of the ladder.
2. You need to be able to get from any rung to the next rung.

If you can do those two things, you can climb as high as you want! You can get on the first rung, and then you can get to the second rung, and then you can get to the third rung, and so on — even though there are infinitely many rungs. If you ask me about the millionth rung, I can get there. It may take a while, but I *could* get there eventually.

Therefore, we have shown a *single* voter can be overruled. Now, if we can show that $n$ voters can be overruled implies that $n+1$ voters can be overruled (and that we have more than $n+1$ voters), then we can conclude that *any* number of voters can be overruled, which means we must have infinitely many voters. This is the essence of mathematical induction.

### To Infinity and Beyond

> **Proposition:** If any strict subset of size $n$ voters can be overruled (implying $V$ contains more than $n$ voters) in an Arrovian voting system, then $V$ must have more than $n+1$ voters, and $n+1$ voters can be overruled with any pair of candidates. \label{induction-step}

\proofref{induction-step} in appendix.

If you read my [Gibbard-Satterthwaite post](../gibbard-satt){:target="_blank"}, this proof construction might seem familiar! However, the precise mechanism is slightly different, as we shall see. Let's go through the logic carefully, as we did before.

Let's go back to our friend Victor, that little scamp. Victor, on this day, has the whim to support Alice then Bob and then Clark. And now, imagine we have a group of diehard Bob supporters from the local University. The Bob Fanclub is *just* small enough that they can be overruled by literally everyone else. Let's say the Bob Fanclub has $n$ voters. The Bob Fanclub hates Alice, but they tolerate Clark. Victor is in agreement with the Bob Fanclub that Bob is better than Clark, but isn't allowed to join the club because he loves Alice.

Unfortunately, it seems that everyone else who is planning to vote besides Victor and the Bob Fanclub are fans of Clark, and hate Bob. However, we don't know if anyone is actually going to show up to vote from that group--they may or may not exist. Let's think about who has to win based on these preferences.

| Voters      | Rankings             |
|-------------|----------------------|
| Victor      | Alice > Bob > Clark  |
| Bob Fanclub | Bob > Clark > Alice  |
| Outsiders   | Clark > Alice > Bob  |

Now, we know that Victor can be overruled. That was the big result from before. And we're assuming the Bob Fanclub is just small enough to be overruled. So what happens?

Well, everyone but Victor agrees that Clark is better than Alice, so society is going to definitely have to rank Clark higher than Alice since he can be overruled by everyone else. Similarly, everyone but the Bob Fanclub agrees that Alice is better than Bob, so society must rank Alice higher than Bob by our assumption that any strict subset of size $n$ can be overruled.

By transitivity, that means society must rank Clark then Alice then Bob, meaning society's ranking matches exactly the Outsiders' ranking. If nobody except Victor and the Bob Fanclub shows up to vote, then that would make no sense! Because if none of the Outsiders show up, then every voter would agree that Bob is better than Clark, so Bob would have to beat Clark. Therefore, at least one Outsider had to show up to vote.

This tells us two important things, based purely on our assumption that any strict subset of size $n$ can be overruled:

- the turnout of the election is strictly bigger than the size of the Bob Fanclub plus one (Victor) ($n+1$)
- and that a set of voters of size $n+1$ can be overruled.

Thus, if $n$ voters can be overruled, then $n+1$ voters can also be overruled, and there must be strictly more than $n+1$ voters in the electorate in order for this to be possible.

Since $n+1$ voters can be overruled, suppose a new member joins the Bob Fanclub. We repeat the scenario again, and come to the same conclusion! Repeating this over and over essentially closes the trap on the idea that the Outsiders are a finite group.

You could start with the Bob Fanclub starting with only a single founder. Do the above logic implies two voters can be overruled. A new member joins, and the Bob Fanclub now has two members. Apply the above logic and now three voters can be overruled. We can add another member and repeat, and so on, and so on.

I think this is a really neat proof. Essentially, we used the fact that a single voter can be overruled, and that a subset of $n$ voters can also be overruled. That implied that society had to rank the candidates the same way as $S'$ (everyone else), overruling the collective set of size $n+1$. For that to make sense, $S'$ has to contain at least one voter, which means we need strictly more than $n+1$ voters in $V$ in order for the argument to go through.

We are now ready to prove Arrow's theorem!

> **Theorem:** (Arrow's Theorem) If $\sigma$ is an Arrovian voting system, then it must be defined on a set of infinitely many voters.

This is certainly not the regular formulation of Arrow's theorem that we saw above, but it is equivalent:

**Proof:** Proof by induction. Lemma \ref{more-than-one} tells us that we must have more than one voter. Proposition \ref{single-block} tells us that any single voter can be overruled. Proposition \ref{induction-step} tells us that if a strict subset of $n$ voters can be overruled, then $V$ must contain more than $n+1$ voters, and that $n+1$ voters can be overruled. Therefore, $V$ cannot contain any finite number of voters. $\square$.

To ensure the logic is clear, we had the proposition that a single voter can be overruled. By the inductive proposition, this tells us that we must have more than $1+1=2$ voters, and that $2$ voters can be overruled. Applying the inductive proposition again tells us we must have more than $3$ voters and that $3$ voters can be overruled. And so on, and so on. If you ask me "what about a set of a billion voters?", then it would take... a while... but I could go from the fact that one voter can be overruled to show that a strict subset of a "billion minus one" voters can be overruled and we must have strictly more than "a billion" voters in $V$.

Thus, an Arrovian voting system could only *possibly* be defined on a set of infinitely many voters. But can it, actually?

## Infinitely Many Voters

Fishburn explains in his paper that, yes, indeed you *can*, in fact, satisfy all of Arrow's criteria so long as you allow infinitely many voters! This gets into the territory of measure theory, which is far beyond the scope of this post. However, I think I can explain the basics at an intuitive level.

I'm going to be a bit fast and loose with the language and properties of measures, the purpose of this section is just to give an intuitive sense of how we can get around Arrow's theorem with infinitely many voters, and not to give a rigorous introduction to measure theory.

We define a function $\rho$ (a "probability measure"), which takes in a subset of voters, and outputs a "measure" of either 0 or 1. If a subset outputs 1 ("has measure 1"), it "has the power". Otherwise, a measure 0 set "does not have the power".

The collection of all "powerful" sets--those with measure 1--turns out to be what mathematicians call an **ultrafilter** on $V$: a consistent, maximal notion of "majority" that generalizes the idea of having more than half the votes to infinite electorates. Arrow's theorem, viewed through this lens, says that the only ultrafilter on a *finite* set is a principal ultrafilter (generated by a single voter), which is exactly a dictatorship. With infinitely many voters, non-principal ultrafilters exist, and those correspond to Arrovian voting systems with no dictator.

We require that the measure of any single voter is 0, or else we would have a dictator. This also corresponds to our proof that any single voter can be overruled. The induction proof that shows any finite set of voters can be overruled also corresponds to the fact the measure has "finite additivity". That is, for a finite subset of voters $U$, we have

$$\rho(U)\leq \sum_{v\in U}\rho(\{v\})=0$$

We require a few basic, but seemingly obvious properties from $\rho$:

$$\rho(V)=1,\rho(\varnothing)=0$$

The set of all voters should absolutely have the power, that's just unanimity! And no voters at all shouldn't have the power, that's just... well, it would be weird if that were the case.

Further, if we split the set of voters in two, then the sum of their measures should add up to the measure of all voters (which is 1):

$$\rho(S)+\rho(V-S)=\rho(V)=1$$

which implies that if $S$ "has the power", then everyone outside of $S$ does not have the power. And if $S$ does not have the power, then everyone outside of $S$ does have the power. We can extend this to partitions of any finite size, but, in particular, we care mostly about size three:

If $V$ can be partitioned into $V=S_1\sqcup S_2\sqcup S_3$, then

$$\rho(S_1)+\rho(S_2)+\rho(S_3)=\rho(V)=1$$

Since $\rho$ can only output 0 or 1, this means exactly one of these sets can "have the power" and no others.

We might understand "having the power" intuitively as, for example, having a "majority" if we had a finite number of voters. But that notion loses a concrete meaning when we have infinitely many voters, so we need to define a more abstract notion of "power" that can be applied to any subset of voters. This is what the $\rho$ function does.

We focus our attention on sets like $\rho(a>b)$, where $\rho(a>b)$ is simply the measure of the set of all voters who rank $a>b$. For any pair of candidates $a,b$, we can split $V$ into three parts:

$$V=\{v:a>_v b\}\sqcup\{v:b>_v a\}\sqcup\{v:a=_v b\}$$

The measures have to add up perfectly,

$$\rho(V)=1=\rho(a>b)+\rho(b>a)+\rho(a=b)$$

Exactly one of these measures, out of $\rho(a>b)$, $\rho(b>a)$, or $\rho(a=b)$ has to equal 1, and the rest zero. We then, simply, decide societal rankings by asking if the voters who give a certain ranking "have the power". Whichever has measure 1 decides how society ranks $a$ and $b$.

> **Definition:** We define our voting function $\sigma$ to rank $a>b$ if and only if $\rho(a>b) > \rho(b>a)$. If $\rho(a>b) = \rho(b>a)$, then we define $\sigma$ to rank $a=b$.

For this defined $\rho$ we're using, this simplifies into society ranking $a>b$ if and only if $\rho(a>b)=1$ and $\rho(b>a)=0$. If they're both equal, they must both be zero, and so $\rho(a=b)=1$. However, this more general definition will come in handy soon!

For each pair of candidates, $x,y$, we ask if the set of all voters who rank $x>y$ "has the power". If they do, then society ranks $x>y$. Otherwise, they don't. If the voters who rank $x=y$ have the power, then society will rank $x=y$. Otherwise, the set of voters who rank $y>x$ must have the power, and society will rank $y>x$ instead.

This is *extremely* abstract. We have infinitely many voters, and we basically isolate each match-up and ask if the voters who vote a certain way have the power or not. We know exactly one of the sets who all rank a certain way has the power, and that allows us to define this without fail. But when we have a finite number of voters, we don't necessarily have a coherent ranking. For example, if we have 3 voters who rank

- 1: $a>b>c$
- 1: $b>c>a$
- 1: $c>a>b$

Then a majority rank $a>b$, $b>c$, *and* $c>a$. Therefore, there's no way to define a coherent ranking. $a>b>c$ doesn't match that $c>a$. We are left in a rock-paper-scissors cycle and can't define a ranking via majority rule. Can our abstract $\rho$ function define a coherent ranking? As it turns out, yes!

We will only have to use a single fact from measure theory to prove this:

$$X\subset Y\cup Z\implies \rho(X)\leq\rho(Y)+\rho(Z)$$

Intuitively, if I "count" everyone who wears a red shirt, and I "count" everyone who wears flip-flops, then the sum is at least as big as the "count" of everyone who wears at least one of those items, due to double counting. So the measure of a set is at most the sum of the measures of two sets that cover it. We extend this notion to abstract measures, based on the subset relation above.

> **Lemma:** The voting system $\sigma$ defined by $\rho$ on a set of infinitely many voters $V$ induces a transitive ranking.\label{infinite-transitive}

**Proof:** If $\rho(a>b)=1$, then $\rho(b>a)=0$ so society can't rank both $a>b$ and $b>a$. We will show that $\sigma$ is transitive, which is the only non-trivial part of being a ranking ("weak order").

Suppose that society ranks $a\geq b$ and $b\geq c$. We need that society ranks $a\geq c$.

If a voter ranks $c>a$, then they must rank either $c>b$ or $b>a$ (this is something good to ponder on and make sure you understand). Thus,

$$\{v: c>_v a\}\subset \{v: c>_v b\}\cup \{v: b>_v a\}$$

By the additivity property of measures, we have that

$$\rho(c>a)\leq \rho(c>b)+\rho(b>a)$$

If society ranks $a\geq b$ and $b\geq c$, then $\rho(b>a)=0$ and $\rho(c>a)=0$. Therefore, the smaller $\rho(c>a)=0$, must also be $0$, so society ranks $a\geq c$. $\square$

Now, the following is actually a much more general proof than what Fishburn proves in his paper, but I realized that I could actually extend it more generally.

> **Theorem:** Suppose that we have a measure function $\rho$ on subsets of some set of voters $V$.
>
> 1. $\rho(\varnothing)=0$, $\rho(V)=1$
> 2. $\rho(\{v\})\leq\frac{1}{2}$ for all $v\in V$
> 3. The relation $$a>_{\sigma}b$$ if and only if $\rho(a>b)>\rho(b>a)$ is transitive.
>
> Then $\sigma$ defined by this relation is Unanimous, non-dictatorial, and satisfies IIA.\label{measure-theory-arrovian}

\proofref{measure-theory-arrovian} in appendix.

The $\rho$ function we've been dealing with on the set of infinitely many voters satisfies all of these properties! In particular, we have that $\rho(\{v\})=0$ is surely less than $\frac{1}{2}$ for any single voter $v$. Since this system allows any possible ranking, we have successfully defined an Arrovian voting system!

> **Theorem:** The voting system $\sigma$ defined by $\rho$ on a set of infinitely many voters $V$ is Arrovian. \label{infinite-arrovian}

**Proof:** By Theorem \ref{measure-theory-arrovian}, $\sigma$ is unanimous, non-dictatorial, and satisfies IIA. By Lemma \ref{infinite-transitive}, $\sigma$ induces a transitive ranking. This construction allows for three or more candidates and voters can submit any possible ranking. Therefore, $\sigma$ is Arrovian. $\square$

### The Approval Voting Exception

From [Wikipedia:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow's_impossibility_theorem)

> "Rated voting rules, where voters assign a separate grade to each candidate, are not affected by Arrow's theorem. Arrow initially asserted the information provided by these systems was meaningless and therefore could not be used to prevent paradoxes, leading him to overlook them. However, Arrow would later describe this as a mistake, admitting rules based on cardinal utilities (such as score and Approval voting) are not subject to his theorem."

The above proof relies on infinitely many voters. But what if I told you that there is actually one pseudo-Arrovian voting system that works for finitely many voters? And it happens to be the *simplest* possible voting system.

> **Definition:** Approval voting is a voting system where voters can "approve" of as many candidates as they find acceptable, and the candidate with the most approvals wins.

We bring this into our measure notation by defining the Approval measure $\rho_A$ on a finite set of voters and their cast approval ballots. For the remainder of this section, we will assume that a voter prefers $a>b$ if and only if they approve $a$ and do not approve $b$. That is the only way to express a strict preference under Approval voting, which greatly simplifies the analysis.

> **Definition:** For a set $V$ with a finite number of voters $n\geq 2$, we define the Approval measure of an arbitrary subset $S$, $$\rho_A(S)=\frac{\lvert S\rvert}{n}$$. We define $\rho_A(a)$ to be the proportion of voters who approve of candidate $a$. We denote $\rho_A(a>b)$ to be the proportion of voters who approve $a$ and not $b$, and $\rho_A(a=b)$ to be the proportion of voters who approve both $a$ and $b$. Hence, $\rho_A(a)=\rho_A(a>b)+\rho_A(a=b)$.
>
> For the approval voting function $\sigma_A$, we define that $$a>_{\sigma_A}b$$ if and only if $\rho_A(a)>\rho_A(b)$. If $\rho_A(a)=\rho_A(b)$, society ranks $$a=_{\sigma_A}b$$. That is, $a$ is ranked above $b$ by society if and only if $a$ gets more approvals than $b$.

Note that this means, by definition, the ranking induced by $\rho_A$ is transitive, because it is based on a simple comparison of proportions of approval counts, which are real numbers in $[0,1]$. Further, we get that $\rho(V)=1$, $\rho(\varnothing)=0$, and $\rho(\{v\})=\frac{1}{n}\leq\frac{1}{2}$ for all $v\in V$ given that $$n\geq 2$$.

This leads to a simple lemma,

> **Lemma:** In Approval voting, society ranks $$a>_{\sigma_A}b$$ if and only if $\rho_A(a>b)>\rho_A(b>a)$.

**Proof:** This follows extremely straightforwardly. Since $\rho_A(a)=\rho_A(a>b)+\rho_A(a=b)$ and $\rho_A(b)=\rho_A(b>a)+\rho_A(a=b)$, we just cancel the common term of $\rho(a=b)$.

$$\rho_A(a)>\rho_A(b)$$

$$\iff \rho_A(a>b)+\rho_A(a=b)>\rho_A(b>a)+\rho_A(a=b)$$

$$\iff \rho_A(a>b)>\rho_A(b>a)$$

$\square$

This is sufficient to prove that $\sigma_A$ is Arrovian at the ballot level.

> **Theorem:** Approval voting is Arrovian at the ballot level.

**Proof:** By definition, $\rho_A(V)=1$ and $\rho_A(\varnothing)=0$. Further, $\rho_A(\{v\})=\frac{1}{n}\leq\frac{1}{2}$ for all $v\in V$ given that $n\geq 2$. We have that $\rho_A(\cdot)$ induces a transitive order of candidates, which satisfies $$a>_{\sigma_A}b$$ if and only if $\rho_A(a>b)>\rho_A(b>a)$. Therefore, $\sigma_A$ is Arrovian at the ballot level by Theorem \ref{measure-theory-arrovian}.

It's important to be clear about what this proof does and does not say.

**Approval voting is NOT exempt from Arrow's theorem with general preferences.** It only satisfies the conditions of Arrow's theorem *at the ballot level*. Or, alternatively, if we assume that the preferences expressed by voters under Approval voting are perfectly accurate, meaning all voters have strict dichotomous (two-tiered) preferences, then Approval voting is fully Arrovian.

However, the key is precisely the dichotomous preferences. When preferences are dichotomous, nothing goes wrong. You can't get IIA violations. You [cannot get a Condorcet cycle](../condorcet-approval){:target="_blank"}. Dichotomous preferences just *work perfectly*.

Further, as mentioned [in the previous post on the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem](../gibbard-satt){:target="_blank"}, Approval voting is also "strategyproof" (under Dichotomous preferences), but not under more general preferences, [as proven by Brams and Fishburn (1978)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1955105).

Ignoring literal dictatorship, Approval voting stands alone as the champion of satisfying properties mentioned in famous impossibility theorems, if you are willing to be *very* generous about how the definition of those properties apply to Approval voting.

Are any of these results reasons to support Approval voting? Obviously not. That would be ridiculous. Icing on the cake for me, as a mathematician, maybe. But unanimity, IIA, and strategyproofness are only satisfied on the *restricted* rankings that voters can submit. That is, in a Dichotomous world, Approval voting is perfect. But we do not live in a Dichotomous world.

It is not difficult to show that for arbitrary rankings, Approval voting fails both strict unanimity and IIA (and strategyproofness). If every voter prefers $a>b$, but approves both, then it is still possible for $a$ and $b$ to tie for first place. Thus, we don't have strict unanimity. And if we allow voters to have more than two tiers of preference, then a voter who prefers $a>b$ could approve of both $a$ and $b$ or neither (based on how they choose to project their multi-tiered rankings to an Approval ballot), so IIA is also not fully satisfied.

However, if we assume that the preferences expressed under Approval voting are perfectly accurate, then Approval becomes a perfect voting system. But they aren't. Humans have nuanced and complicated preferences. However, Approval *is* unique in this way, because dichotomous preferences are the *maximal* domain in which voting systems work perfectly.

You can restrict *further* and get that Choose-one plurality voting is perfect at the ballot level in the same way, but only because that's an arbitrary restriction on Approval voting. Approval voting is special because dichotomous preferences are special.

Does that have value? That's above my pay grade to decide. I think it's neat that the simplicity and straightforwardness of Approval voting make things like societal ordering of candidates, strategy under the framework of "acceptable" or "unacceptable", and pairwise coherence equally straightforward. But I have [other, more pressing reasons](../approval-only){:target="_blank"}, to support Approval voting over ranked systems. Like I said before: "icing on the cake".

## Conclusion

As stated in the introduction, Democracy is not "mathematically impossible". Arrow's theorem simply tells us that if we want a responsive ranked voting system that is immune to irrelevant options screwing with the results, then it's either a dictatorship or we have infinitely many voters. The solution? Well, we either have to accept irrelevant options will screw with the results of an election using ranked ballots sometimes.

We can use a system that is more resistant to these failures, like a Condorcet-consistent method, which only fails IIA when there is no Condorcet winner, rather than a system like RCV, which is a [disaster both theoretically and practically](../ditch-rcv){:target="_blank"}.*Or*... just not use a ranked voting system? [Approval voting is an excellent choice](../practicalapproval){:target="_blank"} too.

Either way, just remember, folks: Democracy is not doomed, and voting is important. As I was writing this, [an election in Boca Raton, Florida was *literally decided by a single vote*](https://bocanewsnow.com/2026/03/13/andy-thomson-wins-is-new-boca-raton-mayor/) (7,568 to 7,567). IIA may be effectively impossible, but a decent voting system is not.

> **Lemma \ref{overrule}:** (Overruling lemma) If $pvq$ in an Arrovian voting system, then for any $r$ distinct from $p,q$,
>
> - (i) $rvq$
> - (ii) $pvr$

[Back to main text](#thm-overrule)

**Proof:** Consider this first profile:

| Voters | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice | 3rd Choice |
|--------|------------|------------|------------|
| $v$    | $q$        | $r$        | $p$        |
| $V'$   | $r$        | $p$        | $q$        |

By the assumption that $pvq$, we must have that society ranks $p>q$, since $V'$ ranks $p>q$. By unanimity, every voter ranks $r>p$, so society must rank $r>p$. By transitivity, society ranks $r>q$, and in total ranks $r>p>q$. Thus, we have that $v$ ranks $q>r$ but $V'$ ranks $r>q$, and society ranks $r>q$. Therefore, we have $rvq$. That is, we showed that $v$ can be overruled with $r$ over $q$.

Consider the similar second profile where we adjust and shift the ranking of $V'$:

| Voters | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice | 3rd Choice |
|--------|------------|------------|------------|
| $v$    | $q$        | $r$        | $p$        |
| $V'$   | $p$        | $q$        | $r$        |

Similarly, by the assumption that $pvq$, we must have that society ranks $p>q$, since $V'$ ranks $p>q$. By unanimity, every voter ranks $q>r$, so society must rank $q>r$. By transitivity, society ranks $p>r$, and in total ranks $p>q>r$. Thus, we have that $v$ ranks $r>p$ but $V'$ ranks $p>r$, and society ranks $p>r$. Therefore, we have $pvr$. That is, we showed that $v$ can be overruled with $p$ over $r$. $\square$

> **Lemma \ref{single-r}:** For any single voter $v$ who ranks $a>_v b$ in an Arrovian voting system, there exists a candidate $r$ such that $bvr$.

[Back to main text](#thm-single-r)

**Proof:** Since $v$ cannot be a dictator, by assumption. We must have some profile $f$ where $v$ ranks $a>b$ but society does not rank $a>b$ (society ranks $b\geq a$).

We construct a new profile $g$ where everyone has the same relative ranking of $a$ and $b$ as in $f$, but we take a third candidate $r$, which must exist since we have three or more candidates, and construct the profile

| Voters | Rankings     |
|--------|--------------|
| $v$    | $a>r>b$      |
| $V'$   | $a>r$, $b>r$ |

The precise placement of $a$ and $b$ in $V'$ may vary from voter to voter, but we always place $r$ below both $a$ and $b$ in all of their rankings.

Basically, we take the profile $f$ where $v$ is "weakly overruled" on $a$ and $b$, and we insert a new candidate $r$ in between $a$ and $b$ for voter $v$, and insert $r$ at the bottom of the ranking for every other voter. This new profile $g$ agrees with $f$ on $\{a,b\}$, so by IIA, society must rank $b \geq a$ in $g$ as well.

By unanimity, society must rank $a$ over $r$. This means society ranks $a>r$ and $b\geq a$, so by transitivity, society ranks $b > r$. Thus, we have that $v$ ranks $r>b$ but $V'$ ranks $b>r$, and society ranks $b>r$. Therefore, we have $bvr$. $\square$

> **Proposition \ref{induction-step}:** If any strict subset of size $n$ voters can be overruled (implying $V$ contains more than $n$ voters) in an Arrovian voting system, then $V$ must have more than $n+1$ voters, and $n+1$ voters can be overruled with any pair of candidates.

[Back to main text](#thm-induction-step)

**Proof:** Suppose that $V$ contains more than $n$ voters, and let $U$ be a strict subset of $V$ containing $n$ voters. Further, suppose that any strict subset of exactly $n$ voters can *always* be overruled with any pair of candidates. Then let $v$ be a voter outside of $U$, and denote $S'=V-(U\sqcup\{v\})$. Meaning $S'$ is all voters besides those in $U$ and $v$ themselves. We allow the possibility that we have only $n+1$ voters and $S'$ is empty. Consider the following profile:

| Voters | Rankings     |
|--------|--------------|
| $v$    | $a>b>c$      |
| $U$    | $b>c>a$      |
| $S'$   | $c>a>b$      |

We know that any single voter can be overruled on any pair of candidates by Proposition \ref{single-block}, and we have that every voter besides $v$ ranks $c>a$. Therefore, since $cva$, then society must rank $c>a$. Similarly, by assumption that a strict subset of size $n$ can be overruled on any pair of candidates, we have that $U$ ranks $b>a$, but everyone outside of $U$ ranks $a>b$. Thus, society must rank $a>b$. By transitivity, we must have that society ranks $c>b$. At this point, we expect society to rank $c>a>b$, exactly as $S'$ does, but we'll see about that.

If $S'$ is empty, then we would only have voters $v$ and those in $U$. So, by unanimity, we would need that society ranks $b>c$, which would be a contradiction to the fact we showed that society ranks $c>b$. We must then have that $S'$ is nonempty, so $V$ must contain strictly more than $n+1$ voters. Therefore, the set of size $n+1$, $U\cup\{v\}$, can be overruled. Since $U,v,a,b,c$ were arbitrary, we have sufficiently shown that *any* set of size $n+1$ can be overruled in the decision of any two candidates. $\square$

> **Theorem \ref{measure-theory-arrovian}:** Suppose that we have a measure function $\rho$ on subsets of some set of voters $V$.
>
> 1. $\rho(\varnothing)=0$, $\rho(V)=1$
> 2. $\rho(\{v\})\leq\frac{1}{2}$ for all $v\in V$
> 3. The relation $$a>_{\sigma}b$$ if and only if $\rho(a>b)>\rho(b>a)$ is transitive.
>
> Then $\sigma$ defined by this relation is Unanimous, non-dictatorial, and satisfies IIA.

[Back to main text](#thm-measure-theory-arrovian)

**Proof:**

Unanimity: If every voter ranks $a>b$, then $\rho(a>b)=\rho(V)=1$, and hence $\rho(b>a)=\rho(\varnothing)=0$. Thus, $\rho(a>b)>\rho(b>a)$ so society ranks $a>b$.

Non-dictatorship: Suppose one voter $v$ ranks $a>b$ and all other voters rank $b>a$. Then $\rho(a>b)=\rho(\{v\})\leq\frac{1}{2}$ and $\rho(b>a)=\rho(V-\{v\})$. Thus,

$$\rho(b>a)=\rho(V)-\rho(\{v\})\geq \frac{1}{2}$$

We then have $\rho(b>a)\geq \rho(a>b)$, so society ranks $b\geq a$. Thus, $v$ is does not dictator the outcome.

IIA: The societal ranking of $a$ and $b$ is determined entirely by the relationship between $\rho(a>b)$ and $\rho(b>a)$. If we adjust a profile such that we preserve the relative order of $a$ and $b$, then that precisely means that the membership, and hence the value, of $\rho(a>b)$ and $\rho(b>a)$ is preserved. Therefore, the societal ranking must be preserved. $\square$
