## Introduction

The recent California governor's race has sparked renewed debate over the state's Top 2 jungle primary system. It seems that everyone and their grandmother is out planning a ballot initiative for a reform of the system, by shoehorning in their favorite voting system, or repealing the system altogether.<d-footnote>For full transparency, I am currently a volunteer on a committee by the Equal Vote Coalition, which is planning to advocate for the adoption of Approval voting in the primary step of California's elections.</d-footnote>

Let's start with the problem. For a while, there was a serious risk that the runoff for California's governor was going to be between two Republicans: Steven Hilton and Chad Bianco. Regardless of your party affiliation or ideology, that is objectively a terrible choice for bright blue California to be represented by a Republican governor, at least in 2026.

In the end, [only one Republican, Hilton, managed to make it to the runoff](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-primary-elections/california-governor-results), getting second place with nearly 25%, while Bianco only managed about 10%. Democrats also managed to largely get their act together, and mostly rallied behind Xavier Becerra and Tom Steyer. However, Steyer who managed a close third place (less than 2% behind Hilton) ultimately fell short. But looking at the other percentages gives a disturbing picture.

Four candidates managed more than 10% of the vote: Becerra (28.1%), Hilton (24.7%), Steyer (22.8%), Bianco (10.2%). While two more candidates managed to get more than two percent: Porter (4.4%) and Mahan (3.5%). Any voters who preferred Steyer over Becerra but voted for Porter, Mahan, or any other nonviable democrat essentially wasted their vote. Given the second slot was decided by less than 2%, it's entirely possible these candidates spoiled the race for Steyer.

This introduces one major problem with a choose-one jungle primary: Candidate egos. These candidates knew they had no chance, but stubbornly continued to run, potentially spoiling the race for the frontrunner Democrat they preferred. 61 candidates were on the ballot in this election, and a number of them had dropped out of the race after the deadline to file. Betty Yee and disgraced former congressman Eric Swalwell got nearly 70,000 votes combined, despite dropping out weeks before the election.

A choose-one jungle primary is similar to a system called Single Non-Transferable Vote (SNTV). In SNTV, each voter casts one vote in a multi-member district, and the candidates with the most votes win the available seats. In this context, the runoff has two seats candidates are competing for. Work by Cox has indicated that if there are $M$ seats available, then at equilibrium the system can support $M+1$ candidates<d-cite key="cox1994sntv"></d-cite>. This essentially happens through Duvergerian abandonment of anyone who isn't in the top $M+1$ candidates, as voters strategically shift their support to those with a realistic chance of winning<d-footnote>M=1 recovers Duverger's law that voters will abandon anyone except the top two in a single-seat choose-one election. For an election with two winners, this expands to supporting three viable candidates.</d-footnote>.

We basically saw this in the California race: three serious candidates emerged to compete for the two spots. But there were also a number of spoiler candidates who remained.

## The Real Issue

The true issue is vote splitting. The purpose of a primary is to winnow the field of candidates so that the general election is not plagued by multiple candidates dividing the vote among similar ideologies. That's why we have party primaries, so that we don't have 8 democrats splitting the vote while the republicans consolidate behind two candidates.

A choose-one jungle is essentially the absolute worst of choose-one voting on display. "What if we have the worst case scenario of choose-one, and just make *that* the election?"

In California and Washington, which are both blue states that use a system like this, there have been near close calls where democrats have been locked out. [In one Washington election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Washington_Public_Lands_Commissioner_election), a Democrat only made it in over a second Republican by less than 50 votes out of nearly two million.

The solution is extremely simple: allow voters to vote for multiple candidates. Empower voters to support the candidates they like, *as well as* a pragmatic frontrunner to truly impact the race. This is Approval voting: same ballot that we currently have, with the restriction of one vote removed. That's all you need.

## The False Solutions

It seems that California will soon face a number of proposed reforms to the current system. Some want to repeal the system entirely, and go back to party primaries. Some want to expand the primary to take the top *three* or *four* candidates and then use a system like Condorcet<d-footnote>Better Choices supports a top 3 where voters in the general vote in each head to head matchup individually. This is not a bad system, in my view, except that it still preserves the choose-one primary.</d-footnote> or Ranked-Choice voting. But these do not solve the underlying issue of vote splitting.

The pitch of the jungle primary was to make everyone's vote count, and to diffuse the polarization and help moderates and third parties gain influence. But this is simply impossible when voters are limited to choosing a single candidate. They can't support the moderate compromise or third party because they have to support a viable frontrunner, or else risk the other major party winning. A number of people I've spoken to have realized the optimal strategy: wait as long as possible to cast their vote, and then pick the most favorable viable candidate.

So long as voters are held hostage by restricting their votes to one, these wonderful moderating/third party benefits will never happen, no matter how many candidates you shove into the general election.<d-footnote>I have heard an intriguing suggestion of a fixed threshold: ex. advance all candidates who clear 40% approval in the primary. This does solve a number of issues! But does not seem to have gained traction. I may write about this in a future post.</d-footnote> The Duvergerian dynamics will expand to accommodate the greater number of spots, while preserving the same problematic dynamics.

Eric Swalwell's campaign imploded over the course of about a week, when allegations of sexual misconduct exploded across the media. It's entirely possible that most of his votes were from early voters who should have waited before the frontrunners were clear.

The reforms I have heard thus far generally include overcomplicating the ballot. Again, there were *61* candidates on the ballot. Any reform is going to likely increase that number. Trying to shoehorn STAR/SCORE voting or rankings would only serve to bloat the ballot unnecessarily. The number of voters who will be intimidated into not voting at all by seeing $61\times 6=366$ bubbles on their ballot--from the option to give 0-5 stars--is going to be nontrivial. Regardless of how "accurate" the VSE of a score method versus Approval is, the practical and logistical costs are prohibitive and could undermine the goal of increasing voter participation and meaningful choice.

## The Virtues of Approval Top 2

The choice to change the primary to Approval voting would address the core issue of vote splitting, while making the most minimal change possible.

One strong advantage is that this system is *already in use* in Louis<d-cite key="sargent2025stlouis"></d-cite>, and has been a great success thus far. Other methods are untested, or have had disastrous results in practice<d-footnote>See, for example, the implementation of Ranked-Choice voting in Alaska. The top four to RCV election in 2022 was widely infamous and left voters highly dissatisfied because of how RCV failed to address vote splitting or the spoiler effect. Because of vote splitting, RCV elected a Democrat to represent the red state of Alaska.</d-footnote>.

The system is also absurdly practical, low friction, and affordable: minimal ballot changes and you only need to adjust a few lines of code in the existing election software to count multiple bubbles instead of rejecting them. Results would also be no slower to be tabulate than they are under the current system. This isn't something completely new or alien--it's a repeal of the restriction of one vote.

Candidates still have to try to appeal to the widest possible base of voters. But now voters can support their favorites, and all the parties and ideologies they want to be viable, while also giving approval to a pragmatic frontrunner without fear of wasting their vote. Broadly acceptable but underestimated candidates will "bubble up" in the approval counts, gaining visibility and increasing their chances of advancing to the runoff. All candidates will see *the full depth* of their support, which gives *everyone* a good idea of what Californians *truly want* but cannot support because their vote is held hostage.

Even if a one votes for both or neither front-runner, the runoff gives every voter an opportunity to distinguish in the runoff step. This means voters can be more generous with their vote than they might otherwise be in a single round system. They can truly support everyone they would be content with, without fear of splitting the vote.

In [a model I'm working on](https://eigentaylor.github.io/satisficing-voter-sim/), the runoff step acts like a correction for misinformed or tired voters and helps ensure that the final outcome better reflects the true preferences of the electorate. These results should be taken with a grain of salt before being considered definitive.

## What about lockouts?

One potential concern I've heard is that implementing this system will lead to a Republican never making it into the runoff ever again. I used to have this concern too, but I've since heard strong arguments that have changed my mind.

For some left-leaning voters this can be a good or bad thing. For some right-leaning voters, it can feel like disenfranchisement. However, I'd like to give an alternate perspective on this.

In November 2026, a democrat and republican will be on the ballot. However, in another sense, the election is already over. I hope you like Xavier Becerra, because unless he suffers a catastrophic accident, he is going to be the next Governor of California and the outcome is already decided. Hilton is not a serious contender, but a sacraficial lamb. If instead the runoff was between Becerra and the other major democrat Tom Steyer, then all voters would have a *meaningful* choice come November.

In such a race, Republicans could act as the moderating force by supporting the less extreme of the two major democrats, thereby influencing the actual outcome. Both Democrats would have to try to appeal to Republican voters and earn their vote.

In the race that is going to happen, it's entirely possible that doubling down on partisan loyalty, and focusing on getting the most vocal and extreme democrats to come out to vote *against* Hilton is going to be the optimal play. As it stands, Becerra has no incentive to govern for all Californians; only the Democrats who are likely to come out to vote.

Approval has a proven moderating effect (through incentives to appeal to the median voter<d-cite key="cox1985approvalEquilibrium"></d-cite>), and a polarization diffusion mechanism. Long-term, it's entirely possible that a moderate Republican who can appeal to Democrats<d-footnote>Republicans may take a leaf out of the notebook of Texas Democrat James Talarico, who presents a broadly appealing religious take on progressivism. He is currently leading to be the first Democratic Texas Senator in decades, as of June 2026.</d-footnote>, or an independent candidate who can attract support from both sides, could successfully compete and even win. The two-democrats problem is not likely to be permanent, since Approval allows voters freedom to actually show support for the candidates who are not *seen* as viable, but actually have broad support.

No system which restricts votes can allow a "nursery" effect where such broad but nonviable candidates can truly see the depth of their support<d-footnote>This was part of the pitch for RCV <em>and</em> the jungle primary, which never really materialized in practice. In both systems, your vote only counts for one candidate at a time, and stays a scarce resource you are pressured to use strategically.</d-footnote>. Approval in the primary allows such desirable candidates to accumulate measurable support which influences the perception of what *is* or *is not* possible for the next election cycle, potentially paving the way for a more diverse and representative candidate pool in the future.

## Conclusion

The simplest possible change to improve the primary system in California is to allow voters to support more than one candidate in the primary election. [We don't need to overhaul the entire electoral system and implement something brand new, but simply *repeal the arbitrary restriction on the number of candidates we can support*](https://clayshentrup.medium.com/the-only-voting-reform-worth-funding-5776beefdd2b). Doing so will eliminate vote splitting, restore genuine voter agency, and fundamentally transform how candidates engage with the electorate. Approval voting is not a radical experiment--it is a proven, pragmatic solution already succeeding in St. Louis<d-cite key="sargent2025stlouis"></d-cite>.

It requires minimal logistical changes while addressing the core pathology of the current system: the forced choice between sincerity and strategy that leaves millions of voters feeling unheard and elections feeling predetermined.

California has a rare opportunity to lead the nation in electoral reform by embracing simplicity over complexity, and by trusting voters with the freedom to express their true preferences. The question is not whether Approval voting can work--it can, and it does--but whether California has the courage to adopt it. As the state faces yet another election cycle where the outcome feels fixed before the campaign truly begins, that question has never been more urgent.
