## Introduction

At the moment, Instant Runoff Voting (IRV) is the most popular alternative voting system in use around the world. The primary problem with IRV, in my view, is not that it is a *bad* voting system, but that it is *usually fine*, but sometimes *indefensibly awful*. While I have other objections, I think the most dangerous aspect of IRV is that is it's biggest issues are rare, and so it lulls people into a false sense of security. In this post I will illustrate some of the more glaring inconsistencies of IRV.

## What is IRV

Instant Runoff Voting is an overcomplicated voting system for a single-winner method. But let's go along with it for now. In IRV, voters rank candidates in order of preference. If a candidate receives a majority of first-preference votes, they are declared the winner. If no candidate achieves a majority, the candidate with the fewest first-preference votes is eliminated. The votes of the eliminated candidate are then redistributed to the remaining candidates based on the next preferences indicated on those ballots. This process continues until a candidate secures a majority.

This sounds reasonable enough, but it has some glaring issues. First, let's look at the most famous IRV failure: Alaska 2022.

## Alaska 2022

In 2022, [Alaska used IRV for a special election for the U.S. House of Representatives](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.04764v3). The major candidates were Sarah Palin (Republican), Nick Begich III (Republican), and Mary Peltola (Democrat). What happened was that the two Republicans split the conservative vote, leading to Peltola leading in the first real round, without getting a majority (it is Alaska after all, so the Democrat is not likely to get a majority). Begich, the more moderate Republican, had the fewest first-preference votes of the three major candidates, and was eliminated. After the Begich votes (which ranked a second candidate) were redistributed, Peltola still kept her lead and won the election.

On the surface, this doesn't seem like a big deal. Sure, this was the first democratic representative from Alaska in 50 years (with the last one being... Nick Begich Sr, Begich's grandfather? Who... went missing and was declared dead fifty years prior. The Begich lore goes deep...). But that's what IRV does, right? It eliminates the weakest candidate and redistributes their votes until someone gets a majority.

Except, that's wrong on multiple counts.

1. Begich was not the weakest candidate. In fact, he beat both Palin and Peltola in head-to-head matchups. This makes him a "Condorcet winner". 52.5% of voters preferred Begich to Peltola, and 61.4% preferred Begich to Palin. Palin, in fact, was a Condorcet loser (meaning she lost head-to-head matchups against both other candidates). This means that Begich was actually the most broadly acceptable candidate, and this strongly weakens Peltola's mandate and legitimacy as the winner. It's no surprise Begich came back and won against Peltola in 2024.
2. Peltola only got 47.5% of the vote in the final round, so she did not get a majority of the votes in any round. Instead, 7.7% of the voters had their votes exhausted (i.e., they did not rank either of the final two candidates). This means that Peltola won with a mere plurality of the votes. This isn't abnormal, this is standard IRV behavior (the majority guarantee is just a misleading lie).

## Problems with IRV

Let's go point by point on the problems with IRV. First, the undeniable issues.

### Path Dependence

IRV is path dependent, meaning that the order in which candidates are eliminated can affect the final outcome. As we saw, Begich would have won if Palin had been eliminated first. This could have been achieved either by changing the elimination criteria or without changing any relative preference of the actual winner. That is, the original result had Peltola > Palin > Begich, since Peltola beat Palin in the final round, and Begich was eliminated first. But if we had flipped less than three thousand votes, which would not have changed any matchups between Peltola and the republicans, would have resulted in Begich > Peltola > Palin, with Begich winning. No preference related to Peltola changed, but Peltola was no longer the winner.

### Administrative Complexity

IRV is an objectively more complex system than simple plurality or approval voting. You need new machines, new ballots, and it takes much longer to count the votes. This increases the cost of running elections, and increases the chance of errors in counting, potentially exacerbated by the path dependence issue mentioned above. This complexity can also make it harder for voters to understand how their votes are counted, potentially reducing trust in the electoral process.

### Legitimacy Loss

As mentioned above, IRV can elect candidates who are not the Condorcet winner. This is a significant issue because the Condorcet winner is the candidate who would win against every other candidate in a head-to-head matchup, meaning that in a Condorcet failure, the actual winner will always have that another was preferred by a majority of voters hanging over their administration. This weakens the legitimacy of the winner, and will anger the supporters of the true Condorcet winner. In their mind, what was the point of ranking the candidates if their ranking was ignored?

### Shortsightedness

IRV only considers the voter's top choice until that candidate is eliminated. This means that a voter's full preference order is not taken into account until their top choice is no longer in the running. This can lead to issues where a voter's second or third choice could have won if a sufficient number of voters had ranked them higher. This is an IIA (Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives) failure.

In the Alaska 2022 case, 56.9% (33,308) of the 58,582 Palin-first voters ranked Begich as their second choice, and only 5.9% (3,457) ranked Peltola second. This means a **majority** of Palin voters would have preferred Begich over Peltola. Their full ranking was **completely ignored**, and because 5,784 more voters ranked Palin first and Begich second (to the 52,798 Begich-first voters), Begich was eliminated, and Peltola won.

If 2,893 (8.7%) of those Palin-first, Begich-second voters had flipped their top two choices, ranking Begich first and Palin second, Begich would have won the election. This is a clear failure of monotonicity: ranking a candidate higher can actually hurt their chances of winning. The election could have thus been swung by a mere 1.53% of voters cast in the election.

#### Is Ranked Choice a good name

No. Calling Instant Runoff Voting "Ranked Choice Voting" is an incredibly misleading name. In the Alaska example, the preferences of Palin first, Begich second voters were completely ignored, and their worst choice (Peltola) won. This is not "ranked choice", this is "plurality with fall-back instructions".

### Spoiler Susceptibility

As we can see from the Alaska 2022 example, IRV is susceptible to spoilers. Sarah Palin was a Condorcet loser who stopped her closer ideological ally Nick Begich from winning. For all its faults, IRV can't elect a Condorcet loser, but Condorcet losers can still result in strictly worse outcomes by eliminating stronger candidates.

Normally, spoilers get last place in the first round and are taken out of the running. This is, arguably, a good thing. That's basically what the elimination process is designed to do. The problem comes when the spoiler has sufficient support to avoid last place, as Palin did. In this case, the spoiler can eliminate a stronger candidate, like a Condorcet winner, and cause a weaker candidate to win.

### Duopoly Safe

Another side of the same issue of spoilers is that IRV makes it very hard for third-party candidates to succeed. The canonical example of a spoiler is Nader in the 2000 U.S. Presidential Election. IRV is a sort of response to this by saying "well, we should have eliminated Nader first, and then his support would transfer and their voices would be heard". But this doesn't strengthen third parties, it weakens them. Instead it just clears the field for the two major parties to continue to dominate.

It also reduces the bargaining power of third-party candidates, who threaten the major party candidates with spoilage. This is arguably a good thing from one perspective, but it also gives the major two parties the ability to more ignore the will of those voters who are so unrepresented by the two major parties that they vote third party. So, yes, you have eliminated Nader type spoilers, but now the Democrats don't have to worry about trying to capture those votes. Instead, they can rely on the fact that such voters would never vote Republican, and so they can afford to ignore them.

This makes IRV what I call a "token reform": it gives the appearance of reforming the system to be more fair and representative, but in reality it just entrenches the existing two-party duopoly even further. Third parties will struggle to gain sufficient traction to finally do better than one of the two major party candidates. In Burlington 2009, the Progressive Party candidate actually won under IRV, but was also not the Condorcet winner, and that lead to significant backlash and the city ultimately repealing IRV for a time.

If your goal is to break the two-party duopoly, IRV is not the system for you, unfortunately. If you thought it was, you've been duped.
