## Introduction

Every person has been in a situation where they had to pick a restaurant to go to with a group of friends. Did you ask them to pick a single restaurant, or did you ask them which of the options they would be okay with?

Have you ever had to pick a time to meet with a group? Maybe coworkers trying to schedule a meeting, or friends trying to pick a date for a party. If so, did you force them to only pick a single time or did you maybe give them a few options and ask them to pick all the ones that work for them?

Have you ever taken a survey that gave you a bunch of options and asked you to check all the ones that apply to you? Did you wish the survey had forced you to pick just one option instead?

If you can see how ridiculous it would be to force people to pick just one restaurant, or just one time, or just one option on a survey, then you already understand the basic idea of Approval voting. It's a system where voters can approve of as many candidates as they like, and the candidate with the most approvals wins. It's really that simple.

No, *really*, it is that simple. You have already grasped the core concept of Approval voting, and maybe just haven't heard it called that before. The concept is absurdly intuitive and straightforward, and it's already a part of your everyday life.

For most group decisions in real life, when there are more than two options, we don't force people to pick just one. We just ask who is okay with each option, and then we pick the one that is okay with the most people. That's Approval voting in a nutshell. Why don't we use it to elect our leaders?

Approval voting is in use in a few places around the world. The United Nations uses it to elect the Secretary-General. St. Louis, Missouri uses it for their municipal elections. Utah's senate district 11 recently used it for a midterm replacement election. And it's been used in organizations and clubs for decades. But it's not used in any national elections, and it's not even used in most local elections. Why is that?

Unfortunately, the primary method of electoral reform that has taken all the oxygen in the room is [ranked choice voting](../ditch-rcv/){:target="_blank"}, which is a much more complex system that has some glaring and horrendous flaws that have manifested in practice in Alaska, Burlington, and Oakland. Approval voting is a much simpler and more intuitive system that actually provides better outcomes, but it has been overshadowed by the more complex, less intuitive, and less effective ranked choice voting.
