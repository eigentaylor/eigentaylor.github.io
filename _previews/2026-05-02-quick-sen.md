## Introduction

I may do a much more in-depth post on Sen's theorem in the future, but for now I just want to give a quick overview of the theorem with a different example than the one Sen uses in his original paper <d-cite key="sen1970paretianliberal"></d-cite>.

Sen's theorem is often overlooked as kind of the "red-headed stepchild" of social choice theory and is often overshadowed by the more famous theorems of Arrow and Gibbard. As a voting theorist, Sen's theorem is... a bit less consequential than Arrow's or Gibbard's theorems.

Arrow and Gibbard deal with IIA and strategyproofness, respectively, which are themselves very desirable properties. Their impossibility theorems essentially say that these desirable properties are too restrictive to be satisfied by any reasonable voting system. Sen's theorem, which deals with "liberalism," is... less obviously desirable.

### Liberalism

> **Definition:** (Liberalism) A social welfare function (a function which takes in individual preferences and outputs a social preference order) satisfies liberalism if every individual is decisive over at least one pair of alternatives.

This is a pretty powerful property. It basically says that every individual can dictate the social preference order over at least one pair of alternatives. I can sleep on my back or on my stomach, and nobody can stop me!

Sen's theorem shows that this above property is incompatible with Pareto efficiency. But that's less impressive than it sounds, because Liberalism is an *absurdly* strong property. Giving every single individual a veto power on *something* would lead to absolute chaos. So Sen weakens the notion to "minimal liberalism", which is like the baby version of liberalism, applying to a minimum of two people. But despite this much weaker property, we still can prove an impossibility.

> **Definition:** (Minimal Liberalism) A social welfare function satisfies minimal liberalism if there exist at least two individuals who are "decisive" over some pair of alternatives.

Intuitively, this captures a kind of "individual rights." If I am one of these "decisive" individuals, we might interpret this as follows:

If I have the freedom of speech, then I am decisive over the pair of alternatives "speak" (S) and "be quiet" (Q). Therefore, if I prefer to speak (prefer $S>Q$), then the social preference order will reflect my preference. That is, if I rank $S>Q$, then society *must* rank $S>Q$ as well.

Another way to interpret this is that I have a kind of "veto power" over the pair of alternatives $S$ and $Q$. If I prefer $S>Q$, then I absolutely prevent $Q$ from being the top alternative in the social preference order.

Similarly, if I choose to prefer $Q>S$, then I force the social preference order to reflect that as well (i.e. $Q>S$ in the social preference order). In this way, I can also "veto" $S$ from being the top alternative in the social preference order. What I say goes! At least for the pair of alternatives I am decisive over.

We can associate a social welfare function with a social choice function (a function which takes in individual preferences and outputs a single winning alternative) by defining the social choice function to select the top-ranked alternative in the social preference order (meaning no other alternative is ranked higher). Therefore, if I am decisive over $S$ and $Q$, and I prefer $S>Q$, then the social choice function *can never output $Q$*.

By saying I prefer $S>Q$, I may not guarantee that $S$ is the top-ranked alternative in the social preference order, but I can guarantee that $Q$ is certainly not. Hence, the idea of "veto power".

### Pareto Efficiency

The other important concept in Sen's theorem is "Pareto efficiency".

> **Definition:** (Pareto Efficiency) A social welfare function satisfies Pareto efficiency if, whenever every individual prefers alternative $A$ to alternative $B$, the social preference order also ranks $A$ above $B$.

Another way to think of efficiency is that if there is a "Pareto improvement" (a strictly better alternative that everyone prefers), then we should never choose the worse alternative. If we have a case where everyone prefers $A$ to $B$, and we somehow end up with $B$ as the top-ranked alternative in the social preference order, then that's sufficient to say that our social welfare function is not Pareto efficient.

The final two concepts we need to understand Sen's theorem are "unrestricted domain" and "transitivity." Unrestricted domain just means that we allow individuals to have any preference order they want (no ballots or preferences are illegal). Transitivity means that if $A>B$ and $B>C$, then we must have $A>C$. Simple, right?

Sen's theorem can be interpreted as saying that if we have a social welfare function that satisfies minimal liberalism, and we have "unrestricted domain" and "transitivity", then we cannot have Pareto efficiency. This is not the typical statement of Sen's theorem, but it is sufficient to prove the theorem. The typical statement is

> **Theorem:** (Sen's Theorem) There is no social welfare function that satisfies minimal liberalism, Pareto efficiency, unrestricted domain, and transitivity.

Sen proves this theorem, and then uses it to show that liberalism and efficiency are *also* incompatible. This follows immediately, because if you can show that minimal liberalism and efficiency are incompatible, then you have also shown that liberalism and efficiency are incompatible, since minimal liberalism implies liberalism.

Like Arrow's theorem, it's of the form "you can't have all of these things at once". But also like Arrow's theorem, the proof is generally structured as showing one of the properties is violated if we have all the others. In this case, we will show that if we have minimal liberalism, unrestricted domain, and transitivity, then we cannot have Pareto efficiency.

Intuitively, this is a relatively intuitive idea. If we have to respect individual rights (minimal liberalism), then we might be left with an outcome which is clearly not optimal. That is, everyone might be happier with a different choice, but that might come at the cost of violating someone's rights. This is the essence of Sen's theorem.

## Proof by Example

We are going to prove Sen's theorem through an example. I personally found Sen's original example to... not really make a lot of sense. So we are going to go through a different example that I think is a bit more intuitive, and more closely relates to the idea of "individual rights" that Sen is trying to capture with minimal liberalism.

We have two individuals, Connor the Conspiracy Theorist ($C$) and Seth the Skeptic ($S$), who work together in the same office. Connor is a strong believer in the "birds aren't real" conspiracy, and he wants to tell Seth all about it. Connor has the right to free speech, so he is decisive over the pair of alternatives

- $T$: "Connor **Tells** Seth that birds aren't real." Connor loves to debate people about the fakeness of avians, and he wants to convince Seth of the "truth".
- $Q$: "Connor, specifically, must keep **Quiet** and be silenced about the 'birds aren't real' conspiracy." He is not allowed to tell anyone in the office that birds are fake, though other people in the office still might tell Seth about it.

Seth, on the other hand, has little patience for conspiracy theories, and he just wants to be left alone. He believes he has the right not to be offended by nonsense, so he is decisive over the pair of alternatives

- $N$: "Seth **Never** hears about the 'birds aren't real' conspiracy from anyone in the office." Connor would still be allowed to spread his theory to other people in the workplace, just not to Seth.
- $T$: "Connor **Tells** Seth about the 'birds aren't real' conspiracy." Seth can choose to allow Connor to debate him about it.

We assume that Seth's right not to be offended by nonsense means that he is *also* decisive over the pair $N$ and $T$. But he can't force Connor to be quiet ($Q$) and not spread the conspiracy to anyone else in the office, so he is not decisive over $Q$.

Connor, however, is not decisive over $N$ because he doesn't have the right to prevent or force other people to bring it up with Seth. He only has the right to decide whether he, himself, talks about it.

This is where Minimal Liberalism starts to run into problems: when people's individual rights conflict with each other. Connor has the right to free speech, which means he can choose (be decisive) either to be silenced ($Q$) or to speak to Seth ($T$). Seth has the right not to be offended by nonsense, which means he should be able to choose (be decisive) either never to hear about the conspiracy from anyone in the office ($N$) or to hear about it from Connor ($T$). But these rights are in direct conflict with each other.

Intuitively, we have an innate sense that the freedom of speech and the right to not be offended by nonsense are contradictory. We are going to mathematically prove that if we must respect both of these rights, then we will end up with a non-optimal outcome.

Let's suppose Seth comes to Human Resources and complains about Connor. HR brings Seth and Connor into the room and tries to figure out what to do. They have to choose between the three alternatives $T$, $Q$, and $N$. HR wants to protect Connor's freedom of speech, but they also want to protect Seth's right not to be offended by nonsense.

Connor has the preference that, most of all, he wants to tell Seth that birds aren't real ($T$). And he also absolutely *hates* the idea that Seth might never hear the "truth" from anyone else in the office ($N$). He is not a fan of being quiet ($Q$), but it's not as bad as Seth never finding out. Connor believes the truth must come out! So his preference order is $T > Q > N$.

Seth, on the other hand, would like never to have to hear about the conspiracy from anyone at work ($N$). However, he is also very principled. He hates misinformation, so he would actually prefer that Connor never spread his theory in the workplace at all ($Q$). It's just not appropriate! Thus, he actually prefers $Q$ to $N$. But, most of all, he hates the idea of Connor specifically being the one to enlighten him ($T$). So his preference order is $Q > N > T$.

We are thus left with

| Person | Preference Order |
|--------|------------------|
| Connor | $T > Q > N$      |
| Seth   | $Q > N > T$      |

We now look at the alternatives that each person is decisive over. Connor is decisive over $T$ and $Q$, so if he prefers $T>Q$, then we must have $T>Q$ in the social preference order. Seth is decisive over $T$ and $N$, so if he prefers $N>T$, then we must have $N>T$ in the social preference order. Therefore, we have $N>T$ and $T>Q$ in the social preference order.

By transitivity, if we have $N>T$ and $T>Q$, then we must have $N>Q$ in the social preference order. That is, society must rank $N>T>Q$. The social choice must, then, be $N$.

From the veto perspective, we have the following:

| Person | Decisive Pair | Vetoed Alternative |
|--------|---------------|--------------------|
| Connor | $T>Q$         | $Q$                |
| Seth   | $N>T$         | $T$                |

We can't ever choose $Q$, because Connor's freedom of speech allows him to veto being silenced ($Q$). We also can't ever choose $T$, because Seth's right to not be offended by nonsense allows him to veto being the one Connor personally enlightens ($T$). Therefore, we are forced to choose $N$: Seth won't have to hear about birds being fake from anyone in the office.

Therefore, to respect both of their rights, the HR department decides to impose $N$: a rule that Seth is not to hear about any of it from anyone in the office, while Connor is still allowed to talk to other coworkers about his theory (but only if Seth doesn't hear about it).

Here's where we run into the problem: Both Connor and Seth prefer $Q$ to $N$. That is, if forced to choose between $Q$ and $N$, both Connor and Seth would prefer $Q$. Thus, there's actually a better alternative that both Connor and Seth would be happier with.

Connor got his worst choice: Seth won't hear about any of it from anyone in the office ($N$). He would be happier if he, specifically, had to keep quiet ($Q$), while others in the office were still allowed to raise the subject with Seth.

Seth is moderately happy: he won't have to hear about it from anyone while at work ($N$), but he would prefer it if Connor were banned from spreading his theory to anyone in the office ($Q$).

Therefore, we have an outcome ($N$) that is Pareto dominated by another outcome ($Q$). By forcing ourselves to respect both of their rights, and satisfy minimal liberalism, we end up with a non-Pareto efficient outcome. This is the essence of Sen's theorem.

### The Cycle Perspective

Another way to conclude the proof is to necessarily impose Pareto efficiency, and then show that we end up with a cycle in the social preference order (contradicting transitivity). If we force Pareto efficiency, then we must absolutely have $Q>N$ in the social preference order. We thus have that

- $Q>N$ (by Pareto efficiency)
- $T>Q$ (by Connor's liberal decisiveness)
- $N>T$ (by Seth's liberal decisiveness)

which creates a cycle.

Alternatively, by the veto perspective, we can say that Pareto efficiency vetoes $N$ (by forcing $Q$ over $N$). Thus,

| Vetoed Alternative | Reason                      |
|--------------------|-----------------------------|
| $T$                | Seth's liberalism on $T>N$  |
| $Q$                | Connor's liberalism on $T>Q$ |
| $N$                | Pareto efficiency on $Q>N$  |

Therefore, no alternative can be chosen without violating someone's rights, or violating Pareto efficiency. Specifically, Connor vetoed the Pareto efficient alternative $Q$.

This example technically only proves the case where the two decisive individuals are decisive over overlapping pairs of alternatives. However, the proof also shows that even if you have two decisive individuals who are decisive over completely disjoint pairs of alternatives, you can still end up with a cycle in the social preference order if you try to impose Pareto efficiency.

I encourage you to try to do that yourself! Suppose that $S$ is actually decisive over a alternatives $N$ and $R$, and see if you can find a preference profile that leads to a cycle in the social preference order when you try to impose Pareto efficiency. You can check your answer in the proof below.

{% proof Formal Proof %}
**Proof of Sen's Theorem:** Suppose we have two individuals, $C$ and $S$, and we have a social welfare function that satisfies minimal liberalism, unrestricted domain, and transitivity. We will show that this social welfare function cannot satisfy Pareto efficiency.

If $C$ and $S$ are decisive on the same pair of alternatives, then we have a direct contradiction. First, we assume that they are decisive over an overlapping pair of alternatives.

Suppose that $C$ is decisive over $T$ and $Q$, and that $S$ is decisive over $T$ and $N$. Suppose that the preference orders are as follows:

| Agent | Preference Order |
|-------|------------------|
| $C$   | $T > Q > N$      |
| $S$   | $Q > N > T$      |

By $C$'s decisiveness over $T$ and $Q$, we have that if $C$ prefers $T>Q$, then we must have $T>Q$ in the social preference order. By $S$'s decisiveness over $T$ and $N$, we have that if $S$ prefers $N>T$, then we must have $N>T$ in the social preference order. Therefore, we have $N>T$ and $T>Q$ in the social preference order.

By transitivity, society must rank $N>Q$. However, Pareto efficiency requires that if both $C$ and $S$ prefer $Q>N$, then we must have $Q>N$ in the social preference order. Therefore, Pareto efficiency is violated.

Next, suppose that $C$ is decisive over $T$ and $Q$, and that $S$ is decisive over $N$ and $R$, all distinct alternatives. Suppose that the preference orders are as follows:

| Agent | Preference Order |
|-------|------------------|
| $C$   | $R > T > Q > N$  |
| $S$   | $Q > N > R > T$  |

Then by $C$'s decisiveness over $T$ and $Q$, we have that if $C$ prefers $T>Q$, then we must have $T>Q$ in the social preference order. By $S$'s decisiveness over $N$ and $R$, we have that if $S$ prefers $N>R$, then we must have $N>R$ in the social preference order.

Pareto efficiency would require that $R>T$ and $Q>N$. However, we already have that $T>Q$, which alongside $R>T$ implies that $R>Q$. We also already have that $N>R$, which alongside $Q>N$ implies that $Q>R$. Together, we have $R>Q$ and $Q>R$, which is a contradiction. Hence, transitivity would be violated. We therefore cannot satisfy Pareto efficiency. $\square$

The veto perspective is still helpful here. The vetoed alternatives are as follows:

| Vetoed Alternative | Reason                      |
|--------------------|-----------------------------|
| $Q$                | $C$'s liberalism on $T>Q$   |
| $R$                | $S$'s liberalism on $N>R$   |
| $T$                | Pareto efficiency on $R>T$  |
| $N$                | Pareto efficiency on $Q>N$  |

The general "geometry" of the proof is really kind of revealed here. We essentially use liberalism and Pareto efficiency to veto all of the alternatives, which leads to a cycle in the social preference order, or a contradiction in choosing a single winning alternative.
{% endproof %}

## The original example

His example was that of a Prude and a Lewd, and what they might decide to do with "Lady Chatterley's Lover". Essentially, they have to decide between

- Nobody reads the book ($N$)
- Only the Prude reads the book ($P$)
- Only the Lewd reads the book ($L$)

The preference orders are as follows:

| Person | Preference Order |
|--------|------------------|
| Prude  | $N > P > L$      |
| Lewd   | $P > L > N$      |

Supposedly, the Prude wants neither of them to read the book. It's filthy! But, if *someone* has to read it, it would be better that the Prude reads it (and is appropriately horrified by the contents) than the Lewd (who would be delighted by the contents).

The Lewd, on the other hand, for some reason, would rather the Prude read the book to "loosen up". But if the Prude won't read the book, then it's better that the Lewd reads it than nobody reading it at all.

I do not like this example because these alternatives are not really mutually exclusive. Why can't both read the book? Further, does it really make sense that the Lewd wants the Prude to read the book more than he wants to read it himself?

I've heard that it's kind of 70's-coded, as like a censorship kind of thing. So, perhaps this post is just modernizing the example to be more 2020's-coded, given that free speech vs. offensive speech is kind of a hot button issue today. Anyway.

## Conclusion

Sen's theorem is controversial, but I won't get into the general criticisms here. Perhaps in a future post. However, the intuitive idea that respecting individual rights, when they conflict with each other, can lead to suboptimal outcomes seems reasonable to me. The takeaway can be that we may occasionally run into a scenario where achieving an outcome that would be better for everyone would require violating someone's rights.

I just don't know if this theorem is as ground-shattering as Arrow's or Gibbard's theorems. But the proof is kind of neat, and it's easy to align it to a story, so there you go.

Hopefully you found the example that I gave to be somewhat intuitive!
