# Introduction

I recently made a post on [The Gibbard Satterthwaite Theorem](../gibbard-satt){:target="_blank"}, which shows that ranked voting systems cannot be strategy-proof. The Gibbard Satterthwaite Theorem is the cousin of the more famous [Arrow's Impossibility Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow%27s_impossibility_theorem){:target="_blank"}, which shows that ranked voting systems cannot satisfy a set of seemingly reasonable properties. In this post, I will give a walkthrough and proof of Arrow's Theorem based on [a very concise 1970 paper by the legendary Peter C Fishburn](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0022053170900153){:target="_blank"}, who also shows that the theorem does not apply when we have infinitely many voters!

Kenneth Arrow received his PhD in 1951 and [co-won a Nobel Prize in Economics in 1972 for "pioneering contributions to general equilibrium theory and welfare theory."](https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/bios/Arrow.html){:target="_blank"}, and is primarily known for his impossibility theorem, which we will prove in this post.

I also highly recommend [this video by Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qf7ws2DF-zk){:target="_blank"}, which gives a great introduction to the theorem and its implications. However, the clickbait title of "Why Democracy Is Mathematically Impossible" is a bit misleading. I would like to be up front about what I believe can and should be said about Arrow's Theorem.

[Arrow himself has said:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow's_impossibility_theorem#Interpretation_and_practical_solutions){:target="_blank"}

> "Most systems are not going to work badly all of the time. All I proved is that all can work badly at times."

Arrow's theorem is a *beautiful* result, but it is not a death sentence for democracy, or even, more specifically, ranked voting systems. I might even call it a magic trick! Like the Gibbard-Satterthwaite Theorem, the underlying idea is "let's suppose that a ranked voting system satisfies a seemingly innocent property, and then show that property is so restrictive it induces the need for a dictator." For Gibbard-Satterthwaite, the property was strategyproofness. For Arrow's Theorem, the killer property is the "independence of irrelevant alternatives" (IIA). Rather than start with a heavy definition, I'd like to explain the loose intuitive idea with a [famous quote by Sidney Morgenbesser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_irrelevant_alternatives#Motivation){:target="_blank"}:

> Morgenbesser, ordering dessert, is told by a waitress that he can choose between blueberry or apple pie. He orders apple. Soon the waitress comes back and explains cherry pie is also an option. Morgenbesser replies "In that case, I'll have blueberry."

The problem with IIA is that it's misleadingly simple. It's fairly easy to construct an example where this property is violated in our familiar choose-one voting system. For example, let's suppose we leave the decision of dessert between Apple and Blueberry pie up to 100 dinner guests:

| Number of Voters | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice |
|------------------|------------|------------|
| 61               | Apple      | Blueberry  |
| 39               | Blueberry  | Apple      |

Simple enough, Apple wins by quite a lot (61% of the vote). However, suppose now the waitress gives our guests the option to pick Cherry. Cherry is very polarizing, and Blueberry supporters despise it. On the other hand, while many Apple supporters also find Cherry detestable, most of them find Cherry to be a very exciting and delectable dish, and decide to support it instead of Apple, despite still preferring Apple to Blueberry. The new rankings are as follows:

| Number of Voters | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice | 3rd Choice |
|------------------|------------|------------|------------|
| 28               | Apple      | Blueberry  | Cherry     |
| 33               | Cherry     | Apple      | Blueberry  |
| 39               | Blueberry  | Apple      | Cherry     |

Taking only the first choices into account, Blueberry gets first place with 39% of the vote, Cherry comes in second with 33%, and Apple now comes in third with 28%. Unfortunately for Apple, Cherry's entry into the race has caused it to lose. Blueberry wins with 39% of the vote, while Apple only gets 28%. Not a single voter changed their mind about how they feel about Apple relative to Blueberry, but the mere presence of Cherry has caused Apple to get fewer votes than Blueberry. We can also see that Cherry had no real chance of winning, since 67% of voters (over two thirds) preferred both Apple and Blueberry to Cherry. But Cherry was able to seed off enough votes from Apple to cause it to come in last place.

And, unfortunately, not even Ranked Choice Voting (RCV) can save Apple here, because it came in last place. By voting for Cherry first, the Cherry-first-Apple-second voters ordered the dessert they liked least, Blueberry. This is the spoiler effect in action. That majority of 61% of voters *still* prefer Apple to Blueberry, but Blueberry wins because of the presence of Cherry.

This is not some abstract or theoretical violation either. This has happened in Ranked-Choice voting! In Alaska's 2022 Special House election, a similar kind of situation occurred. Nick Begich III (R) would have defeated the Democrat, Mary Peltola, in a one-on-one race. However, the presence of Sarah Palin, who, like Cherry, had no chance to win since she lost to every other candidate, split the Republican vote such that Begich was eliminated first. Mary Peltola then defeated Palin in the final round, despite the fact that nearly 60% of voters ranked a Republican first. Out of the over 30,000 voters who ranked Palin first and Begich second, [if less than 3,000 of them swapped to rank Begich first and Palin second](https://substack.com/@whelmedcitizen/p-182659376){:target="_blank"}, then Begich survives the first round and defeats Peltola, despite no voters changing their relative preference of Begich and Peltola.

This is essentially what IIA says should not happen. If we want to know how Apple compares to Blueberry, or Begich compares to Peltola, we should be able to look purely at how voters feel about Apple relative to Blueberry, and not have to worry about how voters feel about Cherry. I like to call IIA a "stability" property. If we mess around with irrelevant candidates, the standing and order of the relevant alternatives should stay "stable".

The problem is, however, that IIA is *extremely* restrictive. Essentially no "reasonable" *ranked* voting system can satisfy IIA. The typical proof of Arrow's Theorem shows that if you have a "responsive" voting system that satisfies IIA, then it can only be responsive to one voter, and that voter is a dictator. Fishburn's proof actually goes a different route, and shows that if you have a "responsive", "stable" (IIA) voting system that *isn't* a dictatorship, then you must have *infinitely many voters*. Which is too mathematically cute for me *not* to write a blog post about!

## Preliminaries

In total, Fishburn identifies six total properties that Arrow's theorem concerns itself with. Loosely, they are as follows:

1. Having a finite number of voters
2. Having three or more candidates
3. Allowing any possible input ranking of candidates (with ties allowed) from all voters as input, and outputting a societal ordering of candidates (with ties allowed)
4. If all voters agree and rank $a>b$, then society should also rank $a>b$ (unanimity)
5. If every voter maintains their relative ranking of $a$ and $b$, then society should maintain the same relative ranking of $a$ and $b$, even if we move around any or all other candidates (IIA)
6. No single voter decides how society ranks one candidate over another. This would be a "dictator".

> **Theorem:** (Arrow's Theorem) The above 6 properties cannot be satisfied simultaneously by any ranked voting system.

The typical proof of Arrow's theorem shows that if you satisfy the first five conditions, then the sixth has to be violated. The essential idea being that IIA requires the system to be so rigid that it can only be responsive to a single voter. Fishburn instead shows that conditions 2 through 5 imply that the first is impossible. If you want to satisfy all those nice properties, you need an infinite number of voters.

I'd like to really make sure the fundamental ideas of these concepts are rock solid, so forgive me for giving them special attention.

We imagine that we have a set of voters $V$, a set of candidates $X$ with at least 3 candidates, and a voting system $\sigma$ that takes in "weak orders" (rankings of candidates that allow for ties) from each voter (denoted as a function like $f$ that takes in a voter $f(v)$ and outputs their ranking) and outputs a "weak order" of the candidates, which we call the "societal ranking".

One key requirement is that we should allow any possible "ranking" of the candidates to be submitted by the voters, and the voting system should be able to spit out a ranking of the candidates. So just because I submit a wacky contrarian ballot shouldn't cause the voting system to catch on fire and cancel the election.

We won't get into the super technical jargon of what it means to be a coherent "ranking with ties", or a "weak ordering". We will just call this a "ranking", which is exactly what you imagine a ranking with ties to be. For that, we need a basic sense of *transitivity*. If I rank Blueberry $\geq$ Apple and Cherry $\geq$ Blueberry, then I should also rank Cherry $\geq$ Apple. A careful reader may nitpick this is technically "negative transitivity", but I do not think anything is lost by simply calling this "transitivity", and a weak ordering a "ranking".

We need at least three candidates to have a non-trivial voting system. With only two candidates, we can just pick the candidate with the most votes, and that voting system is perfectly fine and satisfies any reasonable property we could want. With three or more candidates, things get interesting, and start breaking.

Our voting system $\sigma$ is a function that takes in the voters' preferences and outputs a collective preference. Essentially, you can think of this as taking in everyone's ranked ballots and outputting a single ranked ballot that represents the collective preference of the voters. So we can ask "how does society rank Apple relative to Blueberry?" by looking at the output of $\sigma$.

Now here we come with the three, more technical properties that we want our voting system to satisfy:

1. **Unanimity**: If every voter prefers candidate $a$ to candidate $b$, then society should also prefer $a$ to $b$.
2. **Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA)**: If we move from one profile to another profile, and every voter maintains their relative ranking of $a$ and $b$, then society should also maintain the same relative ranking of $a$ and $b$, even if we move around any or all other candidates.
3. **Non-Dictatorship**: There should not be a single voter such that whenever they rank $a>b$, then so does society. More intuitively, the final result should not just be a single voter's ballot, regardless of what every other voter does.

Now, I personally found the notation used in Fishburn's paper to be a bit... dense. So I will be defining my own notation, [more consistent with my proof of Gibbard-Satterthwaite](../gibbard-satt){:target="_blank"}, which I hope is a bit more intuitive.

### Notation

We denote that voter $v$ ranks $a>b$ as $$a >_{v} b$$. For a subset of voters $U$, we denote $$a >_U b$$ to mean that every voter in $U$ ranks $a>b$.

If the profile in use is clear from context, we denote that the voting system $\sigma$ ranks $a>b$ as $$a >_\sigma b$$. This means that the output of the voting system ranks $a$ above $b$. We can also write $$a \geq_\sigma b$$ to mean that the voting system ranks $a$ above or equal to $b$.

We say that two profiles $f$ and $g$ "agree" or are "equal" on $\{a,b\}$ if every voter's relative ranking of $a$ and $b$ are the same in both profiles. That is, $$a >_v b$$ in $f$ if and only if $$a >_v b$$ in $g$ for every voter $v$. So if we have one profile where a voter ranks $a>b>c$ and then another where they rank $a>c>b$, then those two profiles agree on $\{a,b\}$, because the voter ranks $a>b$ in both profiles. However, if we have a profile where the voter ranks $b>a>c$, then that profile does not agree with the first two profiles on $\{a,b\}$, because the voter ranks $b>a$ in that profile.

We're often concerned with a single voter at a time, $v$. We denote the set of every voter besides $v$ ($V-\{v\}$) as $V'$.

Finally, we denote $S_1\cup S_2$ to be the "union" of the sets $S_1,S_2$. The new set that contains all elements of both sets. We also denote the "disjoint union" $S_1\sqcup S_2$ to specify that $S_1$ and $S_2$, on their own, contain no common elements.

## The Proof

For the proof, we assume that we have an "Arrovian voting function" $\sigma$ on $V$ that satisfies properties 2 through 6. That is,

> **Definition:** We call a voting function $\sigma$ an "Arrovian" voting function on $V$ if $\sigma$ is a voting system that takes in any possible profile of voters' preferences and outputs a societal ranking of three or more candidates, satisfies unanimity and IIA, and for which no single voter in $V$ is a dictator.

This is essentially our ideal "fair" voting system. To show that the 6 properties Arrow and Fishburn define cannot be satisfied simultaneously, we will prove that if $\sigma$ is Arrovian on $V$, then $V$ must have infinitely many voters.

> **Lemma:** If $\sigma$ is Arrovian on $V$, then there is more than one voter in $V$.

**Proof:** If there was only one voter in $V$, they would be a dictator. Therefore, $V$ must have more than one voter. **QED**

> **Definition:** We say that a voter $v$ can be overruled with $a$ over $b$ if whenever every voter except $v$ ranks $a>b$, then society ranks $a>b$, even if $v$ ranks $b>a$. In other words, $v$'s vote cannot flip the $a$ vs. $b$ outcome against the unanimous preference of every other voter. We denote this property $avb$, and say that $v$ cannot block $a$ over $b$.

By IIA, it's sufficient to show that there exists a single "critical" profile where $v$ ranks $b>a$ and both $V'$ and society rank $a>b$. If such a critical profile exists, then no matter how anyone ranks any other candidate in any other profile where $v$ ranks $b>a$ and everyone in $V'$ ranks $a>b$, IIA would guarantee the same societal relative ranking of $a>b$ as this critical profile, since they would have to agree on the relative rankings of $a$ and $b$.

> **Proposition:** In an Arrovian voting system, any single voter can be overruled by all other voters in their preference of any two candidates. That is, for any voter $v$ and any distinct candidates $a$ and $b$, we have $$avb$$ and $$bva$$.\label{singleblock}

We will prove this incrementally.

> **Lemma:** (Overruling lemma) If $$pvq$$ in an Arrovian voting system, then for any $r$ distinct from $p,q$,\label{lem:overrule}
>
> - (i) $$pvr$$
> - (ii) $$rvq$$

Basically, if $v$ can be overruled with $p$ over $q$, then $v$ can also be overruled with $p$ over any other candidate, and any other candidate over $q$.

We do this by essentially inducing a pseudo-Condorcet cycle of $p>q>r>p$. We let $v$ rank $q>r$, and then translate the $V'$ ranking, looping back around, to ensure $V'$ ranks $p>q$, and then attempt to use unanimity and transitivity to insert a third candidate, $r$.

**Proof:** Consider this first profile:

| Voters | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice | 3rd Choice |
|--------|------------|------------|------------|
| $v$    | $q$        | $r$        | $p$        |
| $V'$   | $r$        | $p$        | $q$        |

By the assumption that $$pvq$$, we must have that society ranks $p>q$, since $V'$ ranks $p>q$. By unanimity, every voter ranks $r>p$, so society must rank $r>p$. By transitivity, society ranks $r>q$. Thus, we have that $v$ ranks $q>r$ but $V'$ ranks $r>q$, and society ranks $r>q$. Therefore, we have $$rvq$$. That is, we showed that $v$ can be overruled with $r$ over $q$.

Consider the similar second profile where we adjust and shift the ranking of $V'$:

| Voters | 1st Choice | 2nd Choice | 3rd Choice |
|--------|------------|------------|------------|
| $v$    | $q$        | $r$        | $p$        |
| $V'$   | $p$        | $q$        | $r$        |

Similarly, by the assumption that $$pvq$$, we must have that society ranks $p>q$, since $V'$ ranks $p>q$. By unanimity, every voter ranks $q>r$, so society must rank $q>r$. By transitivity, society ranks $p>r$. Thus, we have that $v$ ranks $r>p$ but $V'$ ranks $p>r$, and society ranks $p>r$. Therefore, we have $$pvr$$. That is, we showed that $v$ can be overruled with $p$ over $r$. **QED**

This tells us that being overruled on just one pair of candidates implies they can be overruled with many other pairs. But the key to this result is that to be Arrovian we need unanimity, independence, and transitivity. The transitivity, in particular, makes this overruling property so strong.

> **Lemma:** For any single voter $v$ who ranks $$a>_v b$$ in an Arrovian voting system, there exists a candidate $$x$$ such that $$bvx$$.\label{singlex}

**Proof:** Since $v$ cannot be a dictator, by assumption. We must have some profile $f$ where $v$ ranks $a>b$ but society does not rank $a>b$ (society ranks $$b\geq a$$).

We construct a new profile $g$ where everyone has the same relative ranking of $a$ and $b$ as in $f$, but we take a third candidate $x$, which must exist since we have three or more candidates, and construct the profile

| Voters | Rankings     |
|--------|--------------|
| $v$    | $a>x>b$      |
| $V'$   | $a>x$, $b>x$ |

The precise placement of $a$ and $b$ in $V'$ may vary from voter to voter, but we always place $x$ below both $a$ and $b$.

Basically, we take the profile $f$ where $v$ is "weakly overruled" on $a$ and $b$, and we insert a new candidate $x$ in between $a$ and $b$ for voter $v$, and insert $x$ at the bottom of the ranking for every other voter. This new profile $g$ agrees with $f$ on $\{a,b\}$, so by IIA, society must rank $$b \geq a$$ in $g$ as well.

By unanimity, society must rank $a$ over $x$. This means society ranks $a>x$ and $b\geq a$, so by transitivity, society ranks $b > x$. Thus, we have that $v$ ranks $x>b$ but $V'$ ranks $b>x$, and society ranks $b>x$. Therefore, we have $$bv x$$. **QED**

Now we can prove Proposition \ref{singleblock}.

**Proof:** Let $v$ be any voter and $a,b$ be any distinct candidates. We want to show that $$avb$$ and $$bva$$. By Lemma \ref{singlex}, we have that $$bvx$$. We apply the Overruling Lemma repeatedly.

- $$bvx$$ directly gives us $$bva$$ by the Overruling Lemma (i).
- $$bvx$$ implies $$avx$$, by the Overruling Lemma (ii).
- By the Overruling Lemma (i), we have $$avb$$. **QED**

Therefore, we have shown that any single voter $v$ can be overruled by all other voters in their preference of any two candidates. This is a whole lot of rigorous math to show the most basic assumption I think anyone would expect from literally any voting system with at least three voters: that if *every* voter except *one* prefers $a$ to $b$, then society should also prefer $a$ to $b$.

A perceptive reader may notice something odd, though. What if there are only two voters? We only said we have more than one. In that case, we get a strange asymmetry, because if $v$ can be overruled by... one other voter, then that other voter can also be overruled by $v$. However, we never said how many voters there are. The next step is to show that if $n$ voters can be overruled, at all, then,

1. We must have more than $n+1$ voters, and
2. $n+1$ voters can be overruled.

We will do this with a trick called "mathematical induction". I know some non-mathematicians read this blog, so I want to make sure that we're all on the same page about what mathematical induction is. However, if you are a proud mathematician who does induction proofs for breakfast, you can [skip to the next section](#infinitely-many-voters).

### A brief introduction to mathematical induction

The idea is quite simple, and I will borrow the explanation of one of the greatest professors I've ever known: If you want to climb an infinite ladder, you need only two things.

1. You need to be able to get on the first rung of the ladder.
2. You need to be able to get from any rung to the next rung.

If you can do those two things, you can climb as high as you want! You can get on the first rung, and then you can get to the second rung, and then you can get to the third rung, and so on — even though there are infinitely many rungs. If you ask me about the millionth rung, I can get there. It may take a while, but I *could* get there eventually.

Therefore, we have shown a *single* voter can be overruled. Now, if we can show that $n$ voters can be overruled implies that $n+1$ voters can be overruled (and that we have more than $n+1$ voters), then we can conclude that *any* number of voters can be overruled, which means we must have infinitely many voters. This is the essence of mathematical induction.

### To Infinity and Beyond

**Proposition:** If any strict subset of size $n$ voters can be overruled (implying $V$ contains more than $n$ voters) in an Arrovian voting system, then $V$ must have more than $n+1$ voters, and $n+1$ voters can be overruled with any pair of candidates. \label{inductionstep}

If you read my [Gibbard-Satterthwaite post](../gibbard-satt){:target="_blank"}, this proof construction might seem familiar! However, the precise mechanism is slightly different, as we shall see.

**Proof:** Suppose that $V$ contains more than $n$ voters, and let $U$ be a strict subset of $V$ containing $n$ voters. Further, suppose that any strict subset of exactly $n$ voters can *always* be overruled with any pair of candidates. Then let $v$ be a voter outside of $U$, and denote $S'=V-(U\sqcup\{v\})$. Meaning $S'$ is all voters besides those in $U$ and $v$ themselves. We allow the possibility that we have only $n+1$ voters and $S'$ is empty. Consider the following profile:

- $v$ ranks: $a>b>c$
- Every voter in $U$ ranks: $b>c>a$
- Every voter in $S'$ ranks: $c>a>b$

We know that any single voter can be overruled by Proposition \ref{singleblock}, and we have that every voter besides $v$ ranks $c>a$. Therefore, society must rank $c>a$. Similarly, by assumption that a strict subset of size $n$ can be overruled, we have that $U$ ranks $b>a$, but everyone outside of $U$ ranks $a>b$. Thus, society must rank $a>b$. By transitivity, we must have that society ranks $c>b$.

If $S'$ is empty, then we would only have voters $v$ and those in $U$. So, by unanimity, we would need that society ranks $b>c$, which would be a contradiction. We must have that $S'$ is nonempty, so $V$ must contain strictly more than $n+1$ voters. Therefore, the set of size $n+1$, $U\cup\{v\}$, can be overruled. Since $U,v,a,b,c$ were arbitrary, we have sufficiently shown that *any* set of size $n+1$ can be overruled in the decision of any two candidates. **QED**

**Theorem:** (Arrow's Theorem) If $\sigma$ is a voting system that satisfies unanimity, IIA, and contains no dictators, then it must be defined on a set of infinitely many voters.

This is certainly not the regular formulation of Arrow's theorem, but it is equivalent, which we will show directly after the proof:

**Proof:** Proof by induction. Lemma \ref{morethanone} tells us that we must have more than one voter. Proposition \ref{singleblock} tells us that any single voter can be overruled. Proposition \ref{inductionstep} tells us that if a strict subset of $n$ voters can be overruled, then $V$ must contain more than $n+1$ voters, and that $n+1$ voters can be overruled. Therefore, $V$ cannot contain any finite number of voters. **QED**.

To ensure the logic is clear, we had the proposition that a single voter can be overruled. By the inductive proposition, this tells us that we must have more than $1+1=2$ voters, and that $2$ voters can be overruled. Applying the inductive proposition again tells us we must have more than $3$ voters and that $3$ voters can be overruled. And so on, and so on. If you ask me "what about a set of a bajillion voters?", then it would take... a while... but I could go from the fact that one voter can be overruled to show that a strict subset of a "bajillion minus one" voters can be overruled and we must have strictly more than "a bajillion" voters in $V$.

Thus, an Arrovian voting system could only *possibly* be defined on a set of infinitely many voters. Fishburn then also shows, in his paper, that if you have infinitely many voters, then you *can* actually define an Arrovian voting system on it using something called a "probability measure". Now, I won't start getting into the ins and outs of measure theory, but I do think I can sufficiently explain the concept intuitively. But it would, I think, be helpful first to take a detour to a more commonly discussed type of restriction on voting systems: the one-dimensional proximity based preference restriction.

## Proximity Preferences

I think the comparison is helpful here. If we suppose that all candidates and voters lie on a one-dimensional axis (ex. left for liberal and right for conservative), and have voters rank candidates based on who is closest to them, then we get a restriction on the possible ranked preferences that voters are able to make.

For example, it's impossible for any voter to rank a middle candidate last. This means that Arrow's theorem does not apply, since we are restricted in what kinds of rankings voters are able to express. But this imposed restriction gives us something important: transitivity.

If we define a voting system on this unidimensional axis by simply taking majority rule in each pairwise match-up, then this is a fully coherent voting system. I am simplifying this excessively, as this is merely an illustrative example. This is actually a much more general concept, but for our purposes, we will define that a voter ranks candidates by their exact (Euclidean) distance.

> **Theorem:** If voters have unidimensional proximity preferences, and we denote $m$ as the location of the median voter's preference, then society ranks $$a\geq b$$ if and only if $$dist(m,a)\leq dist(m,b)$$. Where $$dist(m,a)$$ is the distance between $m$ and the position of candidate $a$.

**Proof:** A voter $v$ will rank $a>b$ if and only if $dist(v,a)<dist(v,b)$. Therefore, the decision to rank $a>b$ depends on a voter's position relative to the midpoint between $a$ and $b$: $$\frac{a+b}{2}$$. If we assume, without loss of generality, that $a$ is positioned to the left of $b$, then

$$a>_v b \iff dist(v,a)<dist(v,b) \iff v<\frac{a+b}{2}$$

Suppose the median voter is positioned $m<\frac{a+b}{2}$. Then we have that at least half of voters are positioned to the left of $\frac{a+b}{2}$, so at least half of voters rank $a>b$. By majority rule, this means $m<\frac{a+b}{2}$ implies that society must rank $a\geq b$. Similarly, if the median voter is to the right of the midpoint, then society will rank $b\geq a$. If the median voter is positioned precisely at the midpoint, $m=\frac{a+b}{2}$, then that implies that $a$ and $b$ will be tied in the societal ranking. Thus,

$$a\geq_{\sigma} b\iff m\leq \frac{a+b}{2}\iff dist(m,a)\leq dist(m,b)$$

**QED**.

Transitivity comes directly from this:

> **Theorem:** The societal ranking of candidates under unidimensional proximity-based preferences forms a weak order.

**Proof:** Suppose that society ranks $$a\geq b$$ and $$b\geq c$$. Then, $dist(m,a)\leq dist(m,b)$ and $dist(m,b)\leq dist(m,c)$. By transitivity of real numbers, $dist(m,a)\leq dist(m,c)$ implies society ranks $a\geq c$. **QED**

The point of this example is to illustrate that if we induce certain restrictions on how voters can rank things, then the societal rankings become coherent. What Fishburn shows is that if we have infinitely many voters, then we can also define a sort of "measure" that allows us to determine societal rankings in a way that satisfies Arrow's criteria. This is a much more abstract and less intuitive way to get around Arrow's theorem, but it is mathematically elegant.

## Infinitely Many Voters

Fishburn explains in his paper that you *can*, in fact, satisfy all of Arrow's criteria so long as you allow infinitely many voters. This gets into the territory of measure theory, which is far beyond the scope of this post. However, I think I can explain the gist at an intuitive level. I'm going to be a bit fast and loose with the language and properties of measures, the purpose of this section is just to give an intuitive sense of how we can get around Arrow's theorem with infinitely many voters, and not to give a rigorous introduction to measure theory.

We define a function $\rho$ (a "probability measure"), which takes in a subset of voters, and outputs a "measure" of either 0 or 1. If a subset outputs 1 ("has measure 1"), it "has the power". Otherwise, a measure 0 set "does not have the power". We require that the measure of any single voter is 0, or else we would have a dictator. We also require that

$$\rho(S)+\rho(V-S)=1$$

which implies that if $S$ "has the power", then everyone outside of $S$ does not have the power. And if $S$ does not have the power, then everyone outside of $S$ does have the power. In the unidimensional case, having the power was basically equivalent to having the majority. But that notion loses a concrete meaning when we have infinitely many voters, so we need to define a more abstract notion of "power" that can be applied to any subset of voters. This is what the $\rho$ function does. One thing to note is that the proof that this measure even *exists* requires the Axiom of Choice (more specifically, Zorn's lemma), which is also far beyond the scope of this post.

We also ensure some basic properties for coherence:

$$\rho(V)=1,\implies\rho(\varnothing)=0$$

The set of all voters should absolutely have the power, that's just unanimity! And no voters at all shouldn't have the power, that's just... well, it would be weird if that were the case.

We focus our attention on $\rho(a>b)$ and $\rho(b\geq a)$, where $\rho(a>b)$ is simply the measure of the set of all voters who rank $a>b$.

We then, simply, decide societal rankings by asking if the voters who give a certain ranking "have the power".

> **Definition:** We define our voting function $\sigma$ to rank $a>b$ if and only if $\rho(a>b)=1$. If $\rho(b\geq a)=1$, then we define $\sigma$ to rank $b\geq a$. If both $\rho(a>b)=0$ and $\rho(b>a)=0$, then we define $\sigma$ to rank $a=b$.

For each pair of candidates, $x,y$, we ask if the set of voters who rank $x>y$ "has the power". If they do, then society ranks $x>y$. If the voters who rank $x>y$ *do not* have the power, then that means society has to rank $y\geq x$. We can then look at the set of voters who rank $y>x$, and maybe society ranks $y>x$ if it has the power. But also, maybe, the set of voters who rank $x=y$ have the power. In that case, then society will rank $x=y$.

This is *extremely* abstract. We have infinitely many voters, and we basically isolate each match-up and ask if the voters who vote a certain way have the power or not. When we have a finite number of voters, we don't necessarily have a coherent ranking. For example, if we have 3 voters who rank

- 1: $A>B>C$
- 1: $B>C>A$
- 1: $C>A>B$

Then a majority rank $A>B$, $B>C$, *and* $C>A$. Therefore, there's no way to define a coherent ranking. $A>B>C$ doesn't match that $C>A$. We are left in a rock-paper-scissors cycle and can't define a ranking via majority rule. Can our abstract $\rho$ function define a coherent ranking? As it turns out, yes!

> **Theorem:** The voting system defined by $\rho$ satisfies unanimity, IIA, contains no dictators, and always outputs a weak ordering of candidates, even when there are three or more alternatives.

We will only have to use a single fact from measure theory:

$$X\subset Y\cup Z\implies \rho(X)\leq\rho(Y)+\rho(Z)$$

Intuitively, if I "count" everyone who wears a red shirt, and I "count" everyone who wears flip-flops, then the sum is at least as big as the "count" of everyone who wears at least one of those items, due to double counting. So the measure of a set is at most the sum of the measures of two sets that cover it. We extend this notion to abstract measures, based on the subset relation above.

**Proof:** We previously mentioned that $\sigma$ is unanimous, since the set of all voters has the power. Thus, if they all agree on $a>b$, then $\rho(a>b)=\rho(V)=1$, then society must agree that $a>b$.

We also have that $\sigma$ contains no dictators, since the measure of any single voter is 0, so no single voter can have the power.

IIA is also satisfied, since the societal ranking of $a$ and $b$ only depends on $\rho(a>b)$ and $\rho(b>a)$, which, by definition, only depend on how voters rank $a$ relative to $b$.

The only thing left to show is that $\sigma$ always outputs a weak ordering of candidates. We will show that $\sigma$ is transitive, which is the only non-trivial part of being a weak order.

Suppose that society ranks $a\geq b$ and $b\geq c$. We need that society ranks $a\geq c$.

If a voter ranks $c>a$, then they must rank either $c>b$ or $b>a$ (this is something good to ponder on and make sure you understand). Thus,

$$\{v: c>_v a\}\subset \{v: c>_v b\}\cup \{v: b>_v a\}$$

By the property of measures, we have that

$$\rho(c>a)\leq \rho(c>b)+\rho(b>a)$$

If society ranks $a\geq b$ and $b\geq c$, then $\rho(c>b)=0$ and $\rho(b>a)=0$. Therefore, the smaller $\rho(c>a)=0$, so society ranks $a\geq c$. **QED**

## Conclusion

I think it's useful to reemphasize the purpose of each of the properties that Arrow's theorem discusses:

1. **Unanimity**: If every voter prefers $A$ to $B$, then society should also prefer $A$ to $B$. This is a basic fairness condition that ensures that if everyone agrees on something, then society should reflect that agreement.
2. **Three or more candidates**: It doesn't require a rocket scientist to decide how to democratically elect two candidates. But once we introduce a third option, it's difficult to aggregate preferences in a purely coherent way.
3. **Allowing any possible input ranking**: We want our voting system to be able to handle any possible set of preferences from the voters, even if they are very strange or contrarian. This is a sort of "robustness" condition that ensures our voting system can handle any situation. If a voter can cancel the election by submitting a wacky ballot, then that's not a very good voting system.
4. **Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA)**: This is the most controversial and restrictive condition. We would, ideally, like to decide how society ranks $a$ and $b$ purely based on how each voter ranks $a$ relative to $b$, and not have to worry about how voters rank other candidates. This is a stability that becomes so restrictive that it essentially forces the voting system to be a dictatorship or to have infinitely many voters.

Across this proof, we have seen that if we want to satisfy all of these conditions, we are forced into a situation where we either have a dictator or infinitely many voters. This is the essence of Arrow's impossibility theorem. It shows that there is no "perfect" voting system, as long as you require something like IIA to be "perfect".

However, there are ways to relax these conditions to get around the impossibility.

- Almost no systems seriously consider dropping the requirement of unanimity or non-dictatorship. These are foundational to feeling like the system is "democratic" in any meaningful sense. We don't elect candidates by alphabetical order, or by the choice of a single voter, so these conditions are pretty much non-negotiable.
- Increasing our electorate to infinitely many voters is mathematically humorous, but completely impractical in the real world.
- Removing the requirement of IIA is what gets us most of the seriously discussed ranked voting systems. Instant-runoff voting (RCV), Borda, Bucklin, and so on, all violate IIA, but are non-dictatorial and unanimous.
- Condorcet methods, in spirit, omit the requirement to choose a winner in all situations (ex. in the rare rock-paper-scissors cycle). When a Condorcet winner, who defeats all candidates by a majority of votes, exists, then this *sort of* satisfies IIA (in the sense that candidate $a$ defeats $b$ purely based on how people rank $a$ and $b$). However, once you get a cycle, IIA is usually violated by the tie-breaking mechanism chosen.
- Some systems relax the 3 or more candidate condition by adding a runoff, where an Arrow's theorem violating system is used to create a new election with just two candidates which has no issues (except how those two candidates were selected in the first place).
- Many systems just drop the ranking format entirely, and work with independent numerical scores for each candidate. SCORE voting and STAR voting fall into this category.

However, plurality and approval voting technically fall into a different category, where we relax the ability to submit all kinds of rankings. They are both a type of "single-ballot nonranked system", which sits at a sort of intersection of scoring and ranked systems. By being able to cast a single vote for one or more candidate, it's both a cardinal scoring system with a range of 0 to 1, and a ranked system with at most two tiers. Plurality allows exactly one vote, while approval allows votes for as many candidates as a voter wishes.

Plurality, in particular, can be seen as a ranked system where a tie for first is restricted, and then we just count the first ranks. This very blatantly fails IIA. Approval is a little more interesting.

### The Approval Voting Exception

From [Wikipedia:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow's_impossibility_theorem){:target="_blank"}

> Rated voting rules, where voters assign a separate grade to each candidate, are not affected by Arrow's theorem. Arrow initially asserted the information provided by these systems was meaningless and therefore could not be used to prevent paradoxes, leading him to overlook them. However, Arrow would later describe this as a mistake, admitting rules based on cardinal utilities (such as score and approval voting) are not subject to his theorem.

However, we can also view Approval as a type of ranked voting system where voters are restricted to ranking between two tiers of preference, and those in the upper tier get a vote, and those in the lower do not. Even from that perspective, Approval would not break Arrow's theorem because it restricts the rankings voters can submit. But I find the proofs that Approval is unanimous and satisfies IIA to be very cute.

To translate what it means for a voter to prefer $a>b$ to the language of approval voting, we can say that a voter prefers $a>b$ if they approve of $a$ and do not approve of $b$. We define the societal ranking via the ranking of total approvals. Thus, society prefers $a>b$ if $a$ is approved by more voters than $b$ is. Society ranks $b\geq a$ if $b$ is approved by at least as many voters as $a$ is (including a tie, between $a$ and $b$ where society will consider them equal).

We define the notation:

- $S(x>y)$ is the proportion of voters who approve of $x$ and do not approve of $y$
- $S(x)$ is the proportion of voters who approve of $x$.
- $S(x=y)$ is the proportion of voters who approve of both $x$ and $y$.

Note that this means $S(x)=S(x>y)+S(x=y)$, since the only way to approve of $x$ is to either approve of $x$ and not $y$, or to approve of both $x$ and $y$. Similarly, we have that $S(y)=S(y>x)+S(x=y)$.

> **Lemma:** The societal ordering of $a$ and $b$ depends purely on the voters who cast ballots with a "strict" preference between $a$ and $b$. That is, ballots that approve $a$ and not $b$ or $b$ and not $a$.

**Proof:** We have that $S(x)-S(y)=(S(x>y)+S(x=y))-(S(y>x)+S(x=y))=S(x>y)-S(y>x)$. Society ranks $x>y$ precisely when $S(x)>S(y)$, which equivalently means that $S(x>y)>S(y>x)$.

Then, we can express the societal ranking of $a$ and $b$ as follows:

$$a>_{\sigma} b\iff S(a>b)>S(b>a)$$

**QED**.

Forgive me for mentioning it, but the following result comes right out of the above Lemma. If we define that candidate $a$ defeats candidate $b$ pairwise if $S(a>b)>S(b>a)$, then we can see that the above Lemma implies that $a$ defeats $b$ if and only if $a$ gets more approvals than $b$ does. This is a nice and cute little result, because it gives us this nice little Corollary I can't help but mention:

> **Corollary:** If candidate $a$ wins the election, by having more approvals than any other candidate, then $a$ is the Condorcet winner induced by the ballots submitted. That is, $a$ would defeat every other candidate in a pairwise match-up based on the ballots submitted. Conversely, any Approval election that has a unique winner must have a Condorcet winner, and that Condorcet winner must be the unique winner of the election.

**Proof:** $a$ wins the election if and only if $S(a)>S(x)$ for every other candidate $x$. Therefore, $S(a>x)>S(x>a)$ for every other candidate $x$. Thus, $a$ would defeat every other candidate in a pairwise match-up based on the ballots submitted. **QED**

This technically makes Approval voting a "**You can't prove it's not Condorcet**" method.

> **Theorem:** Approval voting satisfies unanimity, IIA, non-dictatorship, even with three or more candidates and a finite number of voters.

**Proof:** Non-dictatorship is fairly obviously satisfied if we have two or more voters. If a single voter prefers $a>b$, and we have every other voter approve of $b$ and not approve of $a$, then if there are two voters, then $a$ and $b$ tie, so society does not prefer $a>b$. If there are three or more voters, then $b$ must get more approvals than $a$, so society prefers $b>a$. Therefore, no single voter can be a dictator.

Unanimity is also satisfied, since if every voter prefers $a>b$, then every voter approves of $a$ and does not approve of $b$, so society must prefer $a>b$. $a$ will get 100% of the votes, while $b$ will get 0% of the votes, so society absolutely prefers $a>b$.

IIA is the most interesting part. We have four types of voters to consider, comprising three categories:

1. Voters who approve of $a$ and do not approve of $b$ (prefer $a>b$).
2. Voters who approve of $b$ and do not approve of $a$ (prefer $b>a$).
3. Voters who approve of both $a$ and $b$ (prefer $a=b$).
4. Voters who approve of neither $a$ nor $b$ (prefer $a=b$).

If we are to preserve the relative ranking of $a$ and $b$, we cannot touch the first two types of voters. There is no way to maintain a preference of $a>b$--where a voter approves of $a$ and not $b$--if we change how they approve $a$ or $b$. Similarly, there is no way to maintain a preference of $b>a$ if we change how they approve $a$ or $b$.

Thus, the only possible way to preserve the relative ranking of $a$ and $b$ is to change how voters in the third and fourth categories approve of $a$ and $b$. That is, voters who approve both $a$ and $b$ then approve neither $a$ nor $b$, or voters who approve neither $a$ nor $b$ then approve both $a$ and $b$.

Since the societal ranking depends purely on $S(a>b)$ and $S(b>a)$, changing the approval of voters in the third and fourth categories does not affect the relative ranking of $a$ and $b$. Therefore, IIA is satisfied. **QED**

Further, as mentioned [in the previous post on the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem](../gibbard-satt){:target="_blank"}, Approval voting is also "strategyproof" (under Dichotomous preferences).

Are any of these results reasons to support Approval voting? Obviously not. That would be ridiculous. Icing on the cake for me, maybe. But unanimity, IIA, and strategyproofness are only satisfied on the *restricted* rankings that voters can submit. That is, in a Dichotomous world, Approval voting is perfect. But we do not live in a Dichotomous world ([unless you choose to pretend you do](../avstratproof){:target="_blank"}).

It is not difficult to show that for arbitrary rankings, Approval voting fails both strict unanimity and IIA (and strategyproofness). If every voter prefers $a>b$, then it is still possible for $a$ and $b$ to tie for first place, so we don't have strict unanimity. And if we allow voters to have more than two tiers of preference, then a voter who prefers $a>b$ could approve of both $a$ and $b$ or neither (based on how they choose to project their multi-tiered rankings to an Approval ballot), so IIA is also not fully satisfied.

However, there is still something to be said that Approval satisfies these properties, and is perfectly Condorcet-efficient, based purely on the ballots submitted. Approval *does* have a sense of independence and [pairwise/Condorcet coherence](../consistentcardinal){:target="_blank"} based on the ballots submitted by voters. In order for the societal ordering of $a$ and $b$ to change, that requires voters to decide to go from treating them differently to equally, or vice versa.

Does that have value? That's above my pay grade to decide. I think it's neato for the simplicity and straightforwardness of approval voting to make things like societal ordering of candidates, strategy under the framework of "acceptable" or "unacceptable", and pairwise coherence equally straightforward. But I have [other, more pressing reasons](../practicalapproval){:target="_blank"}, to support Approval voting over ranked systems. Like I said before: "icing on the cake".
